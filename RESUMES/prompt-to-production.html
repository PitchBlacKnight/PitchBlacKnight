The short version

  1. Generation is the cheap part. The expensive part is getting a hundred assets through review without the brand drifting.
  2. Move the approval upstream. Sign off one style plate, then derive every placement from it. That is where the time actually comes from.
  3. Write prompts in layers, so when something fails review you know which layer broke.
  4. Generate places and light. License or shoot the faces. One rule, most of the risk gone.
  5. Measure it. Cost per usable asset and first-pass review rate. Otherwise you are arguing from anecdotes.

03

The production loop

One brief, end to end. The interesting part is not the generating. It is where the approval gate sits.

The ten steps, in order
  1. Build the layout first. Figma frames with live type. Export the widest as an empty composition reference, so you know where the image has to stay quiet.
  2. Write the subject list. Twelve ordinary moments, one line each, before touching a model. This is still art direction.
  3. Generate the style plate. Base and house blocks, neutral subject, about twenty tries. Pick one and circulate it. Get the sign-off here.
  4. Generate the widest ratio only. Every subject at 16:9 against the approved plate, seeds recorded, negative block applied.
  5. Select against the layout, with type on. Never against the bare image.
  6. Reframe, do not regenerate. Generative expand into every other ratio.
  7. Human finish. Type, logo, color to brand tokens, retouch, contrast checked on the final crop.
  8. Provenance. Reverse image search and trademark scan. Screenshots saved.
  9. Submit as one batch, not twelve separate conversations.
  10. Log the metrics, then fold anything review sent back into the blocks.
Where the time actually goes

Generation is a small slice of the hours. The savings come from three places, none of them the model: one plate approved instead of twelve assets, reframing instead of regenerating, and a first-pass review rate high enough that the batch clears in one round. The tools are the easy part. Everyone already has them.

04

Principles

Five rules that settle the arguments below. When a later section looks like it costs time, one of these is the reason it earns it back.

The model opens the territory. A person closes it.

Generation is for range and volume. Selection, cropping, typesetting, color, and retouch stay human. A generated image is a raw plate, not a finished asset.

If it cannot be regenerated, it cannot ship.

Every production asset records enough state that someone else could remake it in eight months. That is not bureaucracy. That is what you need when legal asks where an image came from and the person who made it has left.

Assume every asset will be challenged.

The question here is never just "is it good." It is whether it implies a clinical claim, whether that person looks like a real person, and whether we have the right to use it.

Consistency beats brilliance across a set.

One stunning generation is a screenshot. Thirty that read as one campaign is a capability. Optimize for the thirtieth.

Know when the answer is not AI.

Real talent, licensed stock, and illustration are still correct answers. A workflow with no stopping rule is not a workflow.

Why they are hereEverything downstream traces back to one of these. The naming spec exists because of rule two. The negative library exists because of rule three. The kill criteria are rule five.
05

Prompt architecture

Prompts get built in five layers, the way a design system gets built in tiers. The layers change at different rates, and that is the whole point.

Why layers rather than one long prompt. When an asset fails review you need to know what caused it. A single block gives you one variable and no diagnosis. Layers let you swap [SUBJECT] across twelve assets while the rest holds, and let you fix a compliance problem once, in [NEG], for everything that follows.

# MERIDIAN · Q3 Everyday Care · asset 07 [BASE] editorial documentary photography, 35mm, shallow depth of field f/2.0, natural available light, soft directional window light from camera left, fine grain, unposed candid framing, no studio lighting, no lens flare [HOUSE] warm neutral palette, oatmeal and clay and soft white, one cool accent only, lived-in domestic interiors, mid-morning light, generous negative space upper third for type, subject on left or right third, never centered, calm and ordinary, no aspirational gloss [SUBJECT] a woman in her early sixties at a kitchen table, holding a mug in both hands, mid-conversation looking off-camera, relaxed shoulders, slight smile [TECH] --ar 4:5 --style raw --stylize 150 --seed 774213 --sref MRD-PLATE-03 --v 7 [NEG] --no white coat, scrubs, stethoscope, medical device, pill bottle, medication, hospital, clinic, exam room, IV line, hospital wristband, clinical signage, before-and-after framing, body-part focus, expression of pain or distress, text, logo, watermark, signature

06

The house block

The house block is your brand, written in language a model can act on. Writing one is a real design exercise and it deserves the rigor of a type scale.

  1. Pull twenty images the team already agrees are right. Shipped work, not moodboard aspiration.
  2. Name what they share across light, palette, framing, subject behavior, and environment. Literal descriptions beat adjectives. "Mid-morning window light from one side" beats "warm and inviting."
  3. Name what they never do. If the brand never centers a subject, say so.
  4. Test it. Same subject line, block on and off, four generations each. If a stranger can sort the eight into two piles, it works.
  5. Version and date it, so a look change is traceable to a block change.

The anti-pattern: "beautiful, high quality, professional, award winning, 8k, ultra detailed, cinematic, masterpiece." None of that describes a brand. It describes generic model-pleasing. It raises polish and kills specificity, which is backwards for any brand whose equity is calm and ordinary.

Two jobs, one artifactWrite it as prose a human reads too. A new freelancer who reads the house block understands the brand's eye faster than they would from a PDF guideline.
06b

If this were your brand

So here is yours, drafted from published materials only. No guidelines, no assets, no logo. This is what an afternoon of looking produces, and it is the first thing I would put in front of your team.

  • Type. Display is Reckless Neue at 500. Body is Lexend Deca, from a family designed to improve reading proficiency. For a brand whose homepage says 100M+ Americans have access, that is an accessibility decision made at the typeface level.
  • Photography. Warm domestic interiors, natural window light, unposed and mid-gesture, real texture in fabric and hair. Ordinary rooms. No studio, no gloss.
  • The rule most people miss. The clinician is on the device screen, never in the room. Care arrives through the glass. That is your product proposition expressed as a framing constraint, and it is the single most useful thing to encode in a prompt.
  • Shape grammar. Circle, half-circle, and squircle with one squared corner, snapped to a shared grid. Flat color sits next to photo-masked fields. Never a gradient, never an overlay on the photograph.
  • Attribution. Member stories run under real first names with a results-may-vary disclaimer.
Read this wayEvery line is a generation rule in waiting. That is what reading a brand for AI actually means.

07

The negative library

In an unregulated category, negatives fix hands and text artifacts. Here they carry legal weight. Every one below has a reason, and the reason is what makes it defensible in a review conversation.

Tier 1 · Implied credential and clinical setting

NegativeWhy
white coatA non-clinician in a lab coat reads as an implied credential. Standard review rejection.
stethoscopeSame problem, more portable. Models add them unprompted near any health context.
scrubsImplies clinical staffing you are not depicting or employing.
exam room, clinicImplies in-person care, which contradicts a virtual-care proposition.
clinical signageGenerated signage is illegible pseudo-text and sometimes names a real institution by accident.
Tiers 2 and 3, plus the portable block

Tier 2 · Implied treatment, outcome, condition

NegativeWhy
pill bottle, medicationImplies a treatment claim, sometimes a specific drug. Never generate. Art-direct around it.
medical device, IV lineImplies acuity and diagnosis, and becomes a regulatory surface if a device is recognizable.
before-and-after framingThe most reliable way to imply an outcome claim without writing one.
body-part focusA cropped torso or a scale reads as a condition or a result. Keep subjects whole.
pain or distressDepicts a condition you are not qualified to depict, and reads as fear-based marketing.
visible symptomsGenerated skin conditions and injuries are inaccurate by default.

Tier 3 · Craft and provenance

NegativeWhy
text, letteringUnreliable, and it occasionally reproduces real marks. All type is set by hand.
logo, watermarkInvented marks can collide with real trademarks.

On syntax. The --no form is Midjourney. Firefly uses a separate negative field, and conversational models want a plain sentence. Keep the library in one place and translate per tool.

The rule that makes this real

When an asset is rejected, do not just fix that asset. Add the reason to the library the same day. This is the only part of the standard that should grow every month, and a library that has not changed in a quarter is one nobody is using.

08

Consistency

The difference between a lucky image and a campaign. Four mechanisms, in the order you reach for them.

The style platedo this first, always

Generate one image that nails the look with a deliberately neutral subject. Approve it. Then every production asset references it. The diagram in section 03 is this idea drawn out.

# Step 1 · generate and approve the plate [BASE] + [HOUSE] + "an empty kitchen table by a window, morning light, one mug" [TECH] --ar 1:1 --style raw --stylize 150 --v 7 # Save the winner as MRD-PLATE-03. Record its seed and job URL. # Step 2 · every production asset inherits it [TECH] --sref MRD-PLATE-03 --sw 90 --seed <per-asset> --ar 4:5

Seedsthe reproducibility primitive

  • Exploring: no seed. Let it roam, record anything promising.
  • Refining: lock the seed, change one layer at a time. A controlled experiment, not a slot machine.
  • Producing: seed always recorded. Without it, principle two fails.

Referencesstyle, character, composition

Style reference carries the look across assets. Character reference keeps a recurring person consistent, though read section 12 first. Composition reference is the underused one: feed a rough layout so the model leaves space exactly where the headline goes.

Generate to the layout, not the frame

Most designers generate a nice image and then fight it into the placement. Do it the other way. Build the frame first, export the empty layout as a composition reference, and generate into it. Yield goes up sharply, because "beautiful, but the head is where the logo goes" stops being a category of failure.

Aspect ratiosgenerate wide, crop down

Generate the widest placement, then reframe down with generative expand. Regenerating per ratio gives you a set that drifts. Reframing one approved plate gives you a set that matches, and cuts credit spend by roughly the number of ratios you skipped.

The split that solves most healthcare problems

Generate environments, textures, light, and abstraction. License or shoot the faces. That one rule removes most of the likeness and release risk while keeping the speed, because backgrounds, patterns, and reframing are where the volume actually is.

11

Rights

Three separate questions that usually get collapsed into one.

Can we use it commercially?

Most paid tiers grant commercial rights. Most free tiers do not, or grant them with conditions. A free-tier generation is a fine experiment and a bad production asset. Record which tier produced each shipping asset.

Will the vendor defend us?

Not the same question, and this is what separates the tools. Adobe offers IP indemnification on Firefly output for eligible enterprise plans, which is why it is the default for anything shipping. Most other image models offer no equivalent. That is not a reason to avoid them. It is a reason to use them upstream of production and to know which is which, which is what the model field in section 09 is for.

Does the output contain something we have no right to?

  • Trademarks. Models put logos on clothing and signage unprompted. Scan at 100 percent before review.
  • Real places. Recognizable buildings can carry property releases and trade dress.
  • Artist style. Naming a living artist in a prompt is risk with no creative upside. Describe the technique instead.
The reverse-image check

Run every generated asset through reverse image search before review. Sixty seconds, and it catches the two failure modes that are genuinely expensive: output that closely resembles an existing photograph, and a synthetic face that resembles a real, findable person. Log the result.

12

Synthetic people

The highest-risk, highest-frequency decision in health marketing. It deserves a written position rather than a case-by-case argument.

  1. Faces that carry the message are licensed or shot. If a person is the hero of an asset, they are a real person with a release. No exceptions for speed.
  2. Generated people appear incidentally. At distance, in soft focus, from behind, or cropped below the eyeline, where they read as context rather than as a subject.
  3. Never pair a generated person with a quote, a name, a condition, or an outcome. That combination manufactures a testimonial.
  4. Never depict a generated person as a clinician, regardless of framing.
  5. Reverse-image check every generated face that ships, and log it.
Why this is stricter than the law requires

Because the exposure is lopsided. The upside of a generated hero face is a few hundred dollars and a day of production. The downside is a real person finding their likeness selling a health service, in a category where trust is the product. Saying that out loud is what turns a restriction into a position, and it is the reasoning a reviewer will repeat for you when you are not in the room.

Disclosure. Decide once, at brand level, whether AI-assisted imagery gets labeled and where. Platform policies already require it in some ad formats. Record the decision and its date even if the decision is not to label.

13

Human finish

Principle one, made operational. What never gets left to a model, so that "AI-assisted" never quietly becomes "AI-produced."

  • All typography set by hand in brand type. Generated lettering is never kept, even when it looks right.
  • Logo and lockup placement is manual, to clear-space rules.
  • Color corrected to brand tokens. Output lands near the palette, not on it.
  • Retouch is manual: hands, ears, eyes, teeth, jewelry, patterned fabric, and anywhere two objects meet.
  • Crop decided against the real layout, with live type in place.
  • Contrast verified after the final crop, not before.
Say it in the room"The plate is generated, the finish is ours." Vagueness here is what makes leadership nervous, not the technology.
14

Accessibility

Missing from nearly every AI creative guideline in circulation, and directly relevant to a brand serving this many people.

  • Contrast under type. Generated backgrounds are busy in ways stock is not. Verify 4.5:1 for body copy against the actual pixels, after the final crop.
  • Prompt for the quiet zone. The house block asks for negative space in the upper third for this reason. Cheaper to prompt than to fix.
  • A person writes the alt text, describing what the image is doing in the layout, not what is in it.
  • Watch motion in generated video. Rapid camera moves and morphing transitions are a common artifact and a known trigger.
  • Represent the real member population. Models pull hard toward young, thin, light-skinned, non-disabled subjects. Left alone, a generated library ends up less representative than the stock library it replaced.
The audit that makes the point

Once per campaign, lay every generated person in the set on one board and look at it as a population. Individually defensible assets can add up to an indefensible library. Fifteen minutes, and most teams only discover they needed it after someone outside points it out.

16

Measuring outcomes

If the brief is to improve AI outcomes, the first job is defining what an outcome is. Most teams skip that and then argue about whether AI is working using anecdotes. All six of these come out of the review packet.

MetricDefinitionWhy this one
Yield rateSelected ÷ generatedThe honest measure of prompt quality.
Cost per usable asset(Credits × unit cost) ÷ selectedThe number finance understands. Compare it to a stock license and a shoot day.
Time to first approvableBrief to first asset that clears reviewMeasures the pipeline, not just generation. Catches review being the bottleneck.
First-pass review rateApproved unchanged ÷ submittedTells you whether the negative library is working.
Recipe reuse rateAssets made from a saved recipe with no new iterationThe only metric about the system itself.
Set fidelityBlind 1 to 5 brand rating across a full setCatches drift that per-asset review never sees.

Baseline first. Measure two weeks of the current workflow before changing anything, or every later claim is unfalsifiable. Then pick one target, not six. In a tightening media environment that is almost always cost per usable asset, with first-pass review rate as the guardrail so cost is not bought with rework. Report it monthly in one sentence.

The counterintuitive one

A rising yield rate is not automatically good. If yield hits 80 percent, the team has probably stopped exploring and is only running safe, known-good prompts. Some waste is the cost of finding anything new. Watch yield next to set fidelity: high yield with flat fidelity means the recipes work, high yield with falling fidelity means the team is coasting on one look.

17

Kill criteria

When to stop generating and do something else. Written down in advance, because in the moment the sunk cost of forty generations is very persuasive.

  • Three refinement rounds without hitting a 4 on set fidelity. The direction is fighting the model. Change the tool or the concept, not the prompt a fourth time.
  • The asset needs a specific, recognizable, real person. License or shoot.
  • The asset needs to depict a condition, treatment, or outcome. Art-direct around it. No prompt solves this.
  • Cost per usable asset passes the stock license price. The economics have inverted.
  • Repair time exceeds compositing from licensed elements. Track this honestly. Repair time is invisible in credit spend, and it is where AI workflows quietly lose their advantage.
  • Reverse image search returns a close match. Stop. Do not iterate from that seed. Start from a different plate.
