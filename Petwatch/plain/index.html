PetWatch - from user stories to a product worth trusting

Mikel Rosenthal · Product Design
4-hour exercise 5 documents · flows & maps · 1 working prototype AI-assisted, seams shown

As written, the app lets an owner write out a pet's care schedule and lets a sitter read it. But no one can ever mark anything as done. Add that one ability - a checkbox, and a note of who did it and when - and the app changes from a list of chores into the thing both people actually want: a way to know the pet is okay.

03

The big decisions, and where each is explained

Any design is really a stack of judgment calls. These are the six that matter most here, each with a link to the full reasoning.

AddedLetting people mark tasks done
In the original stories, the owner writes the schedule and the sitter reads it - but nobody can ever say "done, 7:12am, it was me." Without that, the owner never stops wondering, and with two sitters and a pet on medication, it's genuinely unsafe: nobody knows the pill was already given. One small addition changes what the product is. Full argument in the product definition
Worried aboutCheck-offs could feel like being watched
The sitter is doing a favor. If checking tasks off feels like a boss monitoring an employee, it could sour the friendship the product depends on. So the wording treats it as the sitter's own record, never the owner's inspection - and it's the first thing I'd test with real people, with a plan B ready if I'm wrong. How I'd test it
Followed, with a noteOnly existing users can be invited
The brief requires this, so version one honors it. But your mom, your neighbor - the people most likely to watch your pet - aren't on this app yet, so the rule stops the product right where it's needed most. Rather than argue, I designed the friendliest possible "not yet" message and set up a counter, so in a month we'll know exactly how often this rule costs us a user. The full reasoning
Left outChat, payments, star ratings, complex schedules
These two people already text each other, and nobody rates their sister. Every cut has a written reason, plus the specific evidence that would make me reverse it. Deciding what not to build is most of the job. The not-building list
Built the cheap wayStore the rule, not a year of reminders
"Breakfast at 7am, daily" is saved once as a rule, and today's list is worked out on the spot when someone opens the app. The obvious alternative - filling a database with every future breakfast - means nightly cleanup jobs and bugs where the schedule and reality disagree. Weeks of engineering saved by a design choice. How the data works
Kept calmNo alarm-red anywhere the sitter looks
A late walk moves to the top of the list and turns a warm amber. It never turns red, because you don't shout at someone doing you a favor. You can see this on the prototype's Today screen. See it live
04

How the four hours were spent

Hour 1

Reading and re-reading the brief. That's where the missing piece showed up. Decided what makes version one and what doesn't. The one hour no tool can speed up.

Hour 2

The written documents - I outlined and dictated, AI drafted, and then I rewrote everything that came back sounding generic or wrong.

Hour 3 – 3.5

Built the prototype and kept poking at it - checking tasks off, undoing them, trying to break the double-feeding guard - until it all behaved.

Hour 4

This page, the AI log, and an honesty pass over everything: writing down what I didn't get to, because that list matters as much as the rest.

And what's deliberately not here, said plainly: no real users have seen this yet (the research plan is how I'd fix that), most screens exist as sketches rather than finished designs, and there's no design-system documentation. Each gap is named, with when I'd close it, at the end of the build plan.
