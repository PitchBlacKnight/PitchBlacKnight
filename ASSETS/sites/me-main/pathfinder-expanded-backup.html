Act I

The Product

What advisors actually do all day, why the old shape of the work kept failing them, and the decisions behind the shape that replaced it.

01The Stakes

What breaks if nothing changes

A client says "our contact center is failing and our WAN contract is up in ninety days." The advisor has to translate that into a technical requirement, survey a market of dozens of providers, get real pricing, and then sit across a table and recommend a stack the client will spend six figures a year on.

Before Pathfinder, that work lived in an inbox. Requirements in email threads. Provider research in a spreadsheet. Assessments requested by Slack and remembered by whoever remembered. Quotes in attachments named final_v3_REAL.xlsx. Nothing had a state.

The advisor loses time — but worse, the advisor loses the thread. Walk into a client meeting unable to answer "why this provider and not that one?" and the recommendation dies with the relationship.

A recommendation you can't explain is a recommendation you can't sell.

The brief I gave myself: give the advisory process a shape — a single project object that carries its own state, evidence, and reasoning from first conversation to signed contract, so that at any moment the advisor can answer where are we and why this.

02Evidence

What I found

I shadowed advisors through live deals rather than interviewing them about deals — fifteen minutes watching someone reconstruct a quote history from their sent folder tells you more than an hour of them describing their process, because nobody describes the workarounds. They've stopped seeing them.

I paired that with a full interface inventory: every distinct screen state in production, clustered by pattern. Three years of separate teams had layered five primary blues, three button shapes, and mixed type stacks across a tool where advisors present live revenue data to clients.

Inconsistency reads as unreliability.
  • F·01Context loss was the real cost. The expensive moment wasn't doing the work — it was re-finding it. Which assessment was requested, which provider was ruled out, which version of the quote was live.
  • F·02Advisors don't want the answer. They want the argument. A ranked provider list is only useful if the advisor can defend the ranking out loud, in front of a client, without the tool in the room.
  • F·03The in-between states are the product. A deal spends most of its life with assessments in progress and quotes pending. Designing only the happy path would have designed for the rarest moment.
03The Model

One object, five states

Everything else followed from one decision about nouns. The platform had been organised around tools — a fiber tool, a data center tool, a quote tool, each a destination you visited and left. I reorganised it around a single object: the Project.

A project has a client, a technology scope, a location, an owner, and — critically — a state. It moves through five of them, in order:

The second half of the model matters as much as the first. Each state produces evidence — research pulls, completed assessments, uploaded documents, provider shortlists — and that evidence never goes back in a drawer. It lives in a persistent rail alongside the work, so an advisor in the Contract state can still see the October research that led to the shortlist in Configuration.

That's the whole idea, and it's why the interface looks the way it does: the project is the noun, the state is the verb, and the rail is the memory.

04The Decisions

Where it was genuinely hard

Three decisions were contested and could reasonably have gone the other way. Here they are with the alternatives I rejected.

Decision 01 · Shape of the work
A guided sequence, or a free-form workspace?
  • AFree-form workspace Every tool available at once; the advisor imposes their own order.
  • BExplicit five-state sequence, non-blocking The order is shown and tracked, but no step is locked. Chosen.
  • CHard wizard Each step gated until the previous one validates. Maximum consistency, cleanest data.
Why B A was the status quo with better styling — it had already failed, because the flexibility advisors "wanted" was what cost them the thread. C was tempting and wrong: real deals arrive out of order, and a gated wizard would have pushed advisors back into email the first time reality didn't cooperate. The sequence is a spine, not a cage — it tells you where you are without telling you where you're allowed to go.
Decision 02 · Provider comparison
Cards, or a dense table?
  • ACard grid Each provider a tile with logo and summary. Modern, breathable, screenshots well.
  • BDense row table Logo, overview, fit score, differentiator and select — one comparison per line. Chosen.
  • CProgressive disclosure Minimal rows that expand on click.
Why B This is the screen an advisor shares on a video call with a client watching. Cards look better in a portfolio and are worse at the job — comparing across an attribute requires alignment along it, and a grid breaks the scan. C fails the same way with an extra click: when a client asks "how does 8×8 compare to Masergy on support?", the answer has to already be on screen. I optimised for the client in the room, not the reviewer on Dribbble.
Decision 03 · The scored recommendation
How much of the reasoning do we show by default?
  • AScore only Show "Technical Fit 5/5" and trust the model. Cleanest interface by far.
  • BFull breakdown always visible Every input and weight, inline, for every provider.
  • CScore plus a read-aloud differentiator, breakdown one interaction away Chosen.
Why C Finding F·02 settles this. A bare score (A) is a liability in the meeting it exists for. B buries the comparison under its own arithmetic at exactly the moment the screen is shared. The differentiator column does the real work: one sentence in the advisor's language, phrased to be read aloud verbatim — "top rated support, 24/7" — with the inputs on demand for the client who pushes. And the score is never final: a checkbox lets the advisor register a provider the model ranked lower.

Project Information

Where the advisor lives between deals

The design problem

An advisor carries a dozen live projects at once. Home has to answer "what needs me today?" in five seconds without becoming a wall of numbers.

What I did

Revenue by technology as standing context, then the two things that change hour to hour: project files with status, and current standings.

Advisor home — standing context above, live state below.

Configuration

The screen the client sees

The design problem

Turn a market of dozens of providers into a defensible shortlist — on a screen shared live with the client paying for the recommendation.

What I did

One dense comparison row per provider: identity, overview, fit score, a read-aloud differentiator, and a register control the advisor can use to overrule the ranking.

Discovery

Where the evidence gets made

The design problem

Discovery is the longest state and the most likely to stall — assessments sit with solutions engineers for days. The interface has to make waiting legible instead of anxious.

What I did

Suggested tools narrowed by the project's scope on the left; live assessment status on the right, every one showing when it started and where it stands.

Discovery — suggested tools narrowed by scope, assessment status always visible.

Registration

Committing to the shortlist

The design problem

Registration is the irreversible step — it commits the deal to specific providers and starts the clock on partner processes. It has to feel like a decision, not a form submission.

What I did

The register action lives inside Configuration as a per-provider checkbox, so the commitment happens next to the reasoning that justified it. 3 of 12 selected is the confirmation.

IQA Builder — the assessment catalogue, filtered by the project's technology scope.

Contract

The state that has to survive

The design problem

A signed deal isn't the end — it becomes a deployment, then a billing relationship, then a renewal three years later, usually handled by someone who wasn't in the original meeting.

What I did

Contract hands the project to the portfolio view, and the rail — every assessment, every provider ruled out — travels with it. The year-three renewal starts with the original reasoning intact.

Portfolio view — opportunities, deployments, and billing as one continuous life.
The screen that carries the argument

Five details on this screen came out of a research finding rather than a preference.

Designing the score you have to defend

Any product that ranks, scores, or recommends is making a probabilistic claim on a user's behalf — whether or not a model is involved. The design question isn't how accurate is it? It's what does the user have to be able to say when someone challenges it?

06Proof

What testing changed

The five-state model went in front of advisors as a clickable prototype before a line of it was built.

  • HeldThe sequence read as obvious. Advisors described the five states back in their own words without prompting — the strongest available signal that a conceptual model matches the one already in someone's head. They also stopped asking "where's the assessment I ran?", the question that had defined the old process.
  • FailedThe first Configuration table was too quiet. Fit score and differentiator carried the same weight as the overview copy, so advisors scanned past the score to the logo and made a brand-recognition decision — exactly what the screen existed to prevent. I rebuilt the row hierarchy to give the score its own type treatment and the differentiator its own column.
  • Failed"Registration" didn't mean what I thought. I'd treated it as a lightweight confirmation; advisors treated it as a commitment with real consequences. That's why the register control ended up inside Configuration, next to the evidence, instead of on a step of its own.

It then shipped behind a flag to a slice of advisors before general release. [ adoption / time-on-task figures to add ]

Act II

The System

A five-state lifecycle is only coherent if every state is built from the same material. Act I is the product decision; Act II is the infrastructure that let it survive three years, two platforms, and 350 contributors.

07The System

From zero — four pillars

The interface inventory from section 02 did double duty — it diagnosed the product problem and made the business case for a design system in the same breath. Four principles governed everything that followed.

P·01

Credibility through clarity

Quote values, assessment results, and deal data get the strongest hierarchy — no exceptions.

P·02

Tokens before components

Decisions live in the token layer. Components consume intent, never raw values.

P·03

Designed or it isn't done

Loading, empty, error, and disabled states ship with v1. A pattern without failure modes is a prototype.

P·04

Adoption over mandate

Does this make the right way the easy way? That's the test for every governance decision.

Token Architecture & Theming

Three-tier architecture: primitives (raw values) → semantic tokens (intent) → component tokens (where it applies). Engineers consume tokens, not hex codes. When the brand evolves, the system re-themes from the primitive layer — nothing downstream changes.

Components & Data Visualization

Every component shipped with documented anatomy, full state coverage — default, focus, error, disabled, loading — responsive behavior, and accessibility notes. If a state isn't designed, it isn't done: principle P·03, downstream of finding F·03.

Advisors present live revenue data to clients, which makes data visualization the credibility layer rather than decoration. The chart library — line, bar, area, stream, radar, hex — runs on the same tokens as everything else.

Governance & Design-to-Code

A system nobody uses is a wiki page. Every governance decision was tested against one question: does this make the right way the easy way?

  • ModelFederated contribution — anyone could propose; a small core reviewed against documented criteria, so the system grew without rotting.
  • DocsDocumentation as product — usage guidance, do/don't pairs, accessibility behavior. Written for the person shipping at 4pm Friday.
  • VersioningSemantic releases with migration notes — engineering upgraded on their own schedule, without surprise breakage.

Design-to-code parity. The same token name travels from Figma variable (action/primary) to CSS custom property (--action-primary) to React prop (intent="primary"). Designers and engineers are provably talking about the same thing, and drift becomes visible — and fixable — immediately.

The system carried Pathfinder from 2.0 through 3.0 without a redesign — the architecture absorbed the evolution. New surfaces shipped visually consistent on day one. Accessibility findings stopped recurring because the fixes lived in the tokens. The library became the default path for campaign work rather than a mandate, which was always the test.

Honest retro — what I'd do differently

I instrumented too late. I defined the five states, designed them, and only then asked what we'd measure — so the first year of data told us how often each state was visited rather than how long advisors were stuck in one. On every engagement since, the metric gets named before a single flow.

Still unsolved: the handoff to solutions engineering. Discovery stalls when an assessment sits with an SE. I made the advisor's side of that wait legible but never got to design the SE's side — the highest-value work left on the table, and a service design problem rather than a UI one.
