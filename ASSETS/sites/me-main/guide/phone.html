00 — What changes on a phone

He can't see the work. So the structure has to be audible.

On a screenshare, layout does your signposting for you — he can see there are three constraints. On a call, you have to say "there were three." Every beat below is re-cut so the shape of the answer is carried by voice, not layout. And nobody can see your notes. That's an advantage — use it.

Count out loud

signposting

"Two costs." "Three constraints." "One decision." Numbers give him a container to put the answer in, and they're free.

Pause longer than feels right

2–3 seconds

Silence stretches on a call. His questions are intentional — the beat before answering reads as considered, and it stops you over-answering.

Numbers land harder

no competition

"Five primary blues, three button shapes, seven sizes" has nothing to compete with on a call. Say it slowly.

Confirm the format with Angela first
  • Neither her email nor the JD says phone or video. Ask on your prep call — "is this a video call or phone, and is it worth having anything ready to share?"
  • Also ask: how long (30 min is a screen, 60 is a working session), and Stephen's own background — designer-who-codes or engineer-who-designs changes which half you lead with.
  • If it turns out to be video after all, use the full case study page instead. This one is the voice cut.
01

The case study, spoken

Same eight beats, re-cut for the ear. Shorter, explicitly counted, no "as you can see." Roughly seven minutes if he lets you run — and he'll interrupt, which is fine.

01problem
Open on the person, not the system
Advisors at Avant were running six-figure enterprise deals out of their inbox. Requirements in email threads, provider research in spreadsheets, quotes in attachments called final_v3_REAL. Nothing had a state. And the expensive part wasn't doing the work — it was re-finding it.
Let the filename land. It's the funniest thing you'll say and it makes the problem concrete in one word. Then stop and let him react.
02why
Say "two costs"
There were two costs. The advisor lost time — but worse, they lost the thread. And a recommendation you can't explain is a recommendation you can't sell. The second cost was on the interface: three years of separate teams had layered five primary blues, three button shapes and seven sizes into a tool where advisors present live revenue data to clients. Inconsistency reads as unreliability.
Slow down on the count. Five, three, seven. On a call those three numbers are the whole audit and there's nothing on screen competing with them.
03constraints
Say "three constraints" — then actually give three
Three constraints. One, I was the design team, so anything I built had to be maintainable by one person. Two, feature work couldn't stop, so the system had to go in underneath a moving product — there was never a moment to rebuild. Three, it had to serve two audiences with opposite needs: a product team that wanted flexibility, and later a marketing org of non-designers who needed the opposite. The tradeoff I made was shipping fewer options than people asked for. One button, three sizes, five states — when the ask was closer to seven.
This is the beat most candidates skip, and on a call it's also the one that proves the story is real rather than rehearsed. Don't cut it for time.
04thinking
Method, then the number it produced
I started by watching rather than asking. I shadowed advisors through live deals instead of interviewing them about deals — fifteen minutes watching someone reconstruct a quote history out of their sent folder tells you more than an hour of them describing their process, because nobody describes their own workarounds. They've stopped seeing them. Then I made the fragmentation countable: a full inventory of every distinct screen state in production, clustered by pattern. Numbers ended an argument that opinions couldn't.
Natural pull-up point. If he sounds engaged: "I can send you the inventory diagram if it's useful — it's one image." See section 02.
05decisions
Say "one decision about nouns" — the strongest sentence you have
Everything followed from one decision about nouns. The platform was organised around tools — a fiber tool, a quote tool, each a destination you visited and left. I reorganised it around a single object: the Project. It carries a state, and it moves through five of them in order, and evidence gathered in any state stays visible in every state after it. The alternative was improving each tool individually — which would have been faster, and would have left the advisor exactly as lost. Underneath that: primitives stay private, semantics are the public API. That's what let the system survive a major version instead of being replaced by one.
Naming the alternative is what makes it a decision rather than a description. On a call that distinction is the whole beat — he can't see the work, so the reasoning is the evidence.
06engineering
Lead with them being right
The naming was a negotiation, not a handoff. Engineering pushed back on my first token names because they read as design words rather than API names — and they were right. A semantic token is an interface, and interfaces get named by the people who call them. So we settled the convention together before I built the library. Then I specced with theming hooks and states enumerated so implementation wasn't a re-draw, and later wired Dev Mode so they were reading the system rather than a picture of it.
Highest-value beat for this specific role. He's hiring a hybrid — "engineering told me I was wrong and they were right" is the most credible collaboration answer available, and it costs you nothing.
07impact
One number you trust, then the qualitative one
That was the fourth system I'd built from scratch, and the number I trust most is this: it carried the product from 2.0 to 3.0 without a redesign, over three years. That's the durability test for a token layer — it absorbed a major version instead of being replaced by one. Then we extended the same foundation into a marketing library that 350-plus managers shipped from with no designer in the loop. And the one I actually watch for: brand review meetings stopped being about policing basics and started being about ideas.
The last sentence is the one people remember. It's qualitative, it's specific, and it's the only adoption metric that can't be gamed.
08improve
Say "four things" — then be genuinely self-critical
I went back through this file recently with fresh eyes and found four things I'd fix. One: no deprecation path — three generations of naming coexisting, because I built the way in and never the way out. Two: no component token tier, so every per-component exception gets made by hand. Three: the button has no focus state — the token's defined, the input component uses it, the button never picked it up, and I missed it for three years. An automated pass against my own accessibility rules caught it in seconds. And four: my own documentation says forty-three semantic tokens when the collection actually holds sixty-two.
This is the answer that wins it, and it's better on the phone than on a screen — because it's pure substance with nothing to look at. Say it unhurried. It's about fifty seconds and every second earns.
02

Pull-up moments

Five things worth showing for sixty to ninety seconds — never a continuous share. Each has an offer line, the thing itself, and a stop line so you close it cleanly instead of trailing off.

How to offer, on a call
  • If you're on video: "Can I share for about a minute? There's one thing that's much easier to show than describe." Share, show, stop sharing. The stopping is what keeps it from becoming a demo.
  • If you're on the phone: "I can send you a link right now if you want to look while we talk — it's a ninety-second thing." Sending a link mid-call is unusual enough to be memorable and it costs him nothing.
  • Either way, offer once. If he declines, describe it in two sentences and move on. Pushing a demo at someone who said no is the fastest way to read as a person who likes their tools more than the problem.
1 · The skill file~60s · strongest
Offer
"The AI-readiness piece is much easier to show than describe — can I put one file on screen for a minute?"
Show
interview-kit/skill/keystone-ds/SKILL.md — scroll the output contract only. Do not scroll the reference files.
Say
"An output contract, three reference files, and a forced self-check. Two rules make it useful: every generated value carries a comment naming the token it came from, so output is auditable rather than trusted — and it has to state its contrast, focus and target-size checks before it returns anything."
Stop
"That's the whole thing — I'll stop sharing. The short version is: constraints, not vibes."
2 · The staleness check~45s · most dramatic
Offer
"There's a forty-five second thing that shows how I stop the guidance rotting — worth a look?"
Show
Terminal in interview-kit/keystone/ — run node build/metadata.mjs --check, edit a component, run it again, watch it report STALE and exit non-zero.
Say
"Each record carries a checksum of the source it came from. I change the component — and the record reports itself stale and fails the build. That's the difference between documentation that rots silently and documentation that tells you it has."
Stop
"That's a CI gate, not a comment. Stopping the share."
3 · The token pipeline~60s
Offer
"On the platform-agnostic piece — I built a small system that does exactly that. One minute?"
Show
localhost:4321/keystone/docs/index.html — the stat row and the three-tier diagram, then toggle the theme once.
Say
"Tokens in DTCG format, three tiers, one-way flow — and the build fails on a higher-tier reference, so the architecture is enforced by the pipeline rather than by a reviewer. One source, four outputs: CSS, JSON, Swift, and the guidance file, which is generated so it can't drift."
Stop
"Hundred and one tokens, two modes, zero violations. I'll stop there."
4 · The audit finding~60s · pairs with beat 08
Offer
"That focus-state thing I mentioned — I can show you it's real rather than a story."
Show
Figma → ui/button variant grid, then ui/input beside it. Dev Mode annotations are already on both.
Say
"Default, hover, pressed, disabled — no focus. The input right here does have one, and it's using the token that's already defined in the collection. So the system knew about focus; the button just never picked it up."
Stop
"Which is the argument for encoding standards as rules instead of writing them down."
5 · Live generation~90s · only if time is generous
Offer
"If you've got ninety seconds I can run one live — it's the fastest way to see what the skill file actually does."
Show
Claude with the skill on — "Build me a primary button with all its variants and states, as a React component."
Say
"Narrate while it runs — never go quiet. Every value annotated with its source token. Six states from an ask for one. A focus ring nobody requested, because accessibility is a rule rather than a review note."
Skip if
You're under 30 minutes, or the connection is unreliable. A stalled generation on a first call costs more than the demo earns. Items 1 and 2 make the same point in half the time and never fail.
03

Phone mechanics

Small things that decide how you come across when voice is the only channel.

Do
  • Stand up. It changes your breath and your energy, and it's audible. Walk if you think better moving.
  • Count your lists out loud before giving them. "Three constraints" tells him how long to listen.
  • Pause two to three seconds before answering. Longer than feels natural. It reads as considered and it stops you padding.
  • Stop talking at the end of an answer. On a call the silence after a good answer does real work — let him fill it.
  • Smile on the hard questions. It genuinely changes your tone, and the "what would you improve" answer needs warmth or it sounds defensive.
  • Use his name once or twice. Not more. On a call it re-establishes that it's a conversation.
Don't
  • Read. He can't see your notes but he can hear them. Glance at a beat name, then look away and say it.
  • Fill silence. The urge is strongest on a phone call and it's where over-answering comes from. Angela flagged that he prefers concise.
  • Say "as you can see" or "on the screen here." Habitual, and instantly wrong on a call.
  • Trail off. Ends like "…so, yeah" undo a good answer. End on the sentence you meant to end on.
  • Run the demo just because you prepared it. If he's deep in the case study, stay there. The artifacts keep.
  • Take the call on speaker or AirPods if you can avoid it. Wired headset or handset — the audio difference is real and it's the whole channel.
The one advantage phone gives you

Nobody can see your notes. Have this page, the four questions, and the match matrix open the entire call. The only discipline that matters is not sounding like you're reading — which is exactly what practise mode drills. Run it once out loud before you dial.
