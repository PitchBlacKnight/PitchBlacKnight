00 — How to use this

Ask two early. Not all of them at the end.

Questions saved for the last five minutes read as a formality. Two asked during the conversation — right after he describes something — read as genuine interest, and they change what you say next because you actually learn something. Angela flagged curiosity specifically. This is where it shows.

Four is plenty

not ten

Pick the four marked PICK. A long list reads as prepared rather than curious, and you won't get through it anyway.

Listen to the answer

actually

Every question below has a "listen for" line. The answer should change something about how you pitch yourself for the rest of the call.

One should be human

at least

He's screening for someone easy to work with. A question about how the team actually operates does more for that than any technical one.

01

Diagnostic — what's actually there

Ask one of these early. What you learn should reshape everything you say afterwards.

Pick · ask this first
Where do the tokens live today, and what's the path from a Figma variable to production CSS?
RevealsWhether this is a greenfield architecture job, a rescue, or a scale job. Three very different roles wearing the same title. Listen for"It's manual" or "designers hand off a spec" → the pipeline doesn't exist and you're building it. "We have a repo but it drifts" → it's a rescue, and your provenance work is the pitch. "Style Dictionary in CI" → they're mature, and the real job is the AI layer. Then sayWhichever of your three stories matches — the build-from-zero at BCBS, the rescue framing, or the durability of Pathfinder 2.0 → 3.0.
What happens right now when a product team needs a component that doesn't exist?
RevealsThe real culture, faster than anything on the job post. Contribution answers are where design systems teams are honest. Listen for"They file a request and wait" → there's a bottleneck and it's probably the person you'd be joining. "They build it themselves and we absorb it later" → there are shadow components everywhere, which is your inventory pitch. Follow up"And how long does that usually take?" The number tells you whether the backlog is the actual problem.
What broke that made platform-agnostic a priority now?
RevealsThere is always a specific incident behind a strategic bullet. Knowing it tells you what success actually means here. Listen forA licensing or vendor-risk answer is a very different mandate than "our native teams can't consume our tokens." The first is a business decision; the second is an engineering one, and only the second is yours to solve.
02

The unusual bullets

Skill files and AI-assisted workflows are the rarest thing in their JD. Find out whether it's real or aspirational — the answer decides whether you're a contributor or the person defining it.

Pick
On the skill files — is anything running today, or is this the first attempt?
RevealsWhether you're joining an effort or starting one. This is the single most useful thing you can learn in the whole interview. Listen for"First attempt" or "we're figuring it out" → you are ahead of them, and you should say so plainly: "I've been building this for my own work — I'd want to show you what I've got and hear where you'd point it." CarefulIf they do have something running, switch to genuine questions about it rather than presenting yours. Curiosity beats a pitch when they're further along.
When you say AI should generate on-system output — what does failure look like today? What does it produce that you don't want?
RevealsWhether they've actually tried it. People who've run this have vivid, specific complaints. People who haven't answer abstractly. Listen for"It reinvents components we already have" → that's the inventory problem and your metadata layer is the answer. "It uses the wrong values" → that's the rules problem and your skill file is the answer. Two different fixes; don't offer the wrong one.
Who's the consumer you're most worried about — designers, engineers, or the tooling?
RevealsWhere the political weight sits. The answer tells you whose experience you'd be optimising for when the three conflict — and they will. Listen forIf they say "designers," the "preserving an excellent designer experience" clause in the JD is load-bearing and someone senior is nervous about the migration.
03

The team and the hybrid seam

At least one of your four should be from here. It's how you show you're thinking about working with people rather than just shipping.

Pick · the human one
This role sits between design and engineering. Who does it sit closest to day to day — and where has that seam been uncomfortable before?
RevealsHonest answer to "why is this role open." The seam is always uncomfortable somewhere, and asking about it directly signals you've worked in it. Listen forAny hesitation is information. "Design owns it but engineering implements" is a very different day than "we sit in the engineering org." Why it landsIt's the least performative question here. You're asking about friction, not org charts — which is what someone who's actually done the job asks.
What's your own background — did you come to this from design or from engineering?
RevealsWhich half of yourself to emphasise for the rest of the conversation. Ask Angela beforehand if you can, but ask him if you can't. Listen forEngineer-who-designs → lead with the pipeline, the build enforcement, the components. Designer-who-codes → lead with adoption, governance, and the 350 contributors. BonusPeople like being asked about themselves. It's the warmest question on this page and costs nothing.
How big is the team, and is this role replacing someone or adding a capability?
RevealsReplacing → there's an incumbent's shape you'd be measured against. Adding → the role is undefined and you have room to define it. Listen for"Adding" plus a 12-month contract usually means they're testing whether the capability is worth making permanent. That's worth knowing before you talk about the first 90 days.
04

Governance and scale

At a company State Farm's size, this is most of the job. These questions signal that you know that.

How does the design system team sit relative to product teams — central, federated, or embedded?
RevealsHow much of the job is politics. Central means you own everything and become a bottleneck; federated means you spend your time on standards and review. Listen forFederated → your governance and contribution work is the whole pitch. Central → your automation work is, because the bottleneck is throughput.
When you deprecate something, what actually happens to the teams still using it?
RevealsWhether they have a deprecation path at all. Most don't — and you have a genuinely good story about discovering that in your own system. Listen forSilence or "we mostly don't" → say your three-generations-of-naming story. It'll land hard, because you found it in your own work rather than criticising theirs.
How do you know today whether the system is being adopted?
RevealsWhether measurement exists. It's a preferred qual in their JD, which usually means they know they should and don't. Listen forIf the answer is anecdotal, offer the distinction that separates you: coverage is depth, usage is breadth, and detachment isn't automatically bad — on a component built to be customised it may mean it's working.
05

Closing

Pick · ask last
A year from now, what would make you say this hire clearly worked?
RevealsHis actual success metric, in his own words — which almost never matches the JD's "success looks like" section. ThenEcho it back in your thank-you note, in his phrasing. It's the highest-return sentence you can write afterwards. Why lastIt's a natural closing question and it leaves the conversation on him imagining you in the role.
Is there anything about my background that gives you pause? I'd rather address it now than have it sit there.
RevealsThe objection you'd otherwise never get to answer. RiskOnly ask if you're steady enough to hear it without getting defensive. If he says "the contract history" or "how much code you write," you have prepared answers — but you have to deliver them calmly, not fast. Why it worksIt's a confident question. People who ask it are usually people who aren't worried about the answer, and that reads.
06

Don't ask these

  • "What's your tech stack?" — you can find most of it out by asking a better question, and it reads junior.
  • "What's a typical day?" — the JD already has a Day-to-Day section. Asking signals you skimmed.
  • Rate, hours, or extension terms. That's Angela's conversation, not his. Raising it with the hiring manager makes the wrong thing the last thing he remembers.
  • "Do you have any concerns?" phrased vaguely — either ask it directly, as in section 05, or not at all.
  • Anything you could have answered by reading their JD. He wrote it.
The four to actually use
  • Early: "Where do the tokens live today, and what's the path to production CSS?"
  • Mid: "On the skill files — is anything running today, or is this the first attempt?"
  • Mid: "Who does this role sit closest to day to day, and where has that seam been uncomfortable?"
  • Last: "A year from now, what would make you say this hire clearly worked?"
