00 — How Stephen runs this

He's evaluating how you think, not what you shipped.

Angela's read: he asks intentional questions, prefers concise answers over long ones, and wants collaborative, humble, curious. With nineteen years behind you, the risk in this room isn't being underqualified — it's sounding like someone who has already figured everything out. Section 03 is where you fix that

Be direct

answer, then stop

He prefers concise over thorough. Answer in three sentences, then stop talking and let him steer. If he wants depth he'll ask — and then you go deep.

Pause first

2 seconds

Angela flagged that his questions are intentional. Take a beat before answering and make sure you're answering the question he asked, not the one you prepared for.

Stay curious

out loud

Name what you're actively learning and what you don't know yet. Nineteen years of range reads as closed unless you deliberately open it.

The two habits to break for this conversation
  • Don't lead with breadth. Your instinct will be to show range — Verizon, GE, BCBS, Avant. Pick one and go deep. Breadth is on the resume; depth is what he's testing.
  • Don't narrate screens. "Here's the dashboard" tells him nothing. Every screen you show should be attached to a decision you made and a reason you made it.
  • Say "I don't know" at least once, if it's true. It's the fastest credibility you can buy with someone who values humility, and it makes everything else you claim more believable.
01

The eight parts

Angela spelled out exactly what Stephen wants covered. Three of these are the ones candidates skip — and they're where he's actually evaluating you.

01

The problem you were solving

Concrete and human. Not "the system was inconsistent" — what was actually going wrong for a person doing their job.

02

Why it mattered

The business cost. If nothing changed, what broke and who paid for it.

03

Constraints and tradeoffs

What you couldn't do, what you gave up, and what you'd have done with more room.Most candidates skip this — it's the first place he'll find out whether it's a real story

04

Your thinking process

How you got from "something's wrong" to "here's the shape of the fix." The messy middle, not the tidy summary.

05

Decisions and why

Named decisions with named alternatives. "I chose X over Y because Z" — the alternative is what makes it a decision rather than a description.

06

How you collaborated with engineering

Specific: what you argued about, what they pushed back on, what changed because of them.He's hiring for a hybrid role — this part is the job

07

What impact it had

Numbers where you have them, honest qualitative where you don't.

08

What you'd improve

Real, specific, and self-critical.The single highest-value answer in the interview — see section 03

Pathfinder case study · top
Technology advisors at Avant were running six-figure enterprise deals out of their inbox. Requirements in email threads, provider research in spreadsheets, quotes in attachments named final_v3_REAL. Nothing had a state. And the expensive part wasn't doing the work — it was re-finding it. An advisor would walk into a client meeting unable to answer "why this provider and not that one," and the recommendation died with the relationship.

03constraints
Nothing — talk to him
Three real ones. I was the design team, so anything I built had to be maintainable by one person. Feature work couldn't stop, so there was never a moment to rebuild — the system had to go in underneath a moving product. And it had to serve two audiences with opposite needs: a product team who wanted flexibility, and later a marketing org of non-designers who needed the opposite, hard rails. The tradeoff I made was deliberately fewer options than people asked for. One button, three sizes, five states — when the ask was closer to seven.

This is the beat most candidates skip. Don't. It's where a real story separates from a rehearsed one.

05decisions
Click "Model" — the five-state rail
Two decisions carried everything. The first was about nouns. The platform was organised around tools — a fiber tool, a quote tool, each a destination you visited and left. I reorganised it around a single object: the Project, which carries a state and moves through five of them in order. Evidence gathered in any state stays visible in every state after it. The alternative was improving each tool individually, which would have been faster and would have left the advisor exactly as lost. The second decision was underneath: primitives stay private, semantics are the public API. That's what let the system survive a major version change instead of being replaced by one.
Short versionOne decision about nouns — the platform was organised around tools, I reorganised it around a single Project object with five states. The alternative was improving each tool individually, which was faster and would have left the advisor just as lost.

The naming was a negotiation, not a handoff. 03

"What would you improve?"

The highest-value ninety seconds in the interview. Most people answer this with a soft non-answer. You can answer it with an audit you actually ran on your own system last week.

The opener that changes the room
I went back through this file recently with fresh eyes, and I found three things I'd fix — which is a slightly uncomfortable answer, but it's the honest one.
  • Nobody expects a candidate to have re-audited their own case study. It reframes you from someone presenting past work to someone still working on it.
  • It's the humility Angela flagged, delivered as competence rather than as an apology.
One · No deprecation path
The variable collection has three generations of naming living side by side — a current lowercase semantic layer, an older PascalCase set, and some bare unnamespaced names from before either. Same values, three conventions. Each generation got added and none got retired, because I built a contribution path and never built a deprecation path. Governance is usually described as how things enter a system. The harder half is how they leave.
Short versionThree generations of naming coexisting, because I built a contribution path and never built a deprecation path. Governance is mostly about how things leave, and I only solved how they enter.
Two · No component tier
The token layer is three collections — Tailwind primitives at 269 variables, Global Tokens for brand at 51, and Semantic Tokens at 62 carrying the Light and Dark modes. That part I'd defend. What's missing is the tier above it: component tokens, so a button's background is button.bg.default pointing at a semantic rather than the component consuming the semantic directly. It sounds like indirection until you need to change one component without touching everything else that shares its semantic. Every per-component exception here is currently made by hand.
Short versionThree token collections, but no component tier — so every per-component exception gets made by hand instead of by the architecture.
Three · A gap the tooling found, not me
The button component has default, hover, pressed and disabled — and no focus state. The focus token is defined in the collection, the input component uses it correctly, the button just never picked it up. I didn't catch that in three years of ownership. A generation pass against my own rules caught it in about four seconds. Which is the honest argument for encoding standards as machine-readable rules rather than documenting them: I'm the person who wrote the accessibility standard for that system, and I still shipped a component that missed it.
Short versionThe button has no focus state. The token's defined, the input uses it, the button never picked it up — and I missed it for three years. A rules pass caught it in four seconds.
Four · The documentation drifted 44% and nobody noticed
The User Guide page inside my own file documents "Semantic Tokens (43 vars)." The collection actually holds 62. The system grew almost half again past its own documentation and nobody caught it, including me. That isn't carelessness — it's what hand-written documentation does by default. It's also the clearest argument I have for generating guidance from the same source as the tokens instead of maintaining it separately, which is exactly what your JD is describing.
Short versionMy own docs say 43 semantic tokens. There are 62. Hand-written documentation rots by definition — which is the argument for generating it from the same source as the code.
Why this answer wins
  • All four are real, checkable, and specific. He can open the file and verify every one.
  • Each one implies a capability rather than just a flaw — deprecation governance, three-tier architecture, machine-readable standards. Every weakness names a thing you now know how to do.
  • The last two are directly the job he's hiring for. You've turned "what would you improve" into the argument for the role's central premise.
04

How you'd actually work

He'll ask some version of "how would you approach this." Have a shape, not a philosophy. This is the loop, not a phased plan.

01

Make it countable before you argue

Inventory first, always. Which components exist, which are used, which got detached, where the raw values are. You don't win the case for a system with taste — you win it with a number nobody can dispute.

Avant five primary blues, three button shapes, seven sizes → one token source of truth
02

Fix the foundation before the surface

Tokens before components, components before templates. A beautiful component on an unstable token layer is rework waiting to happen — and the order is what makes theming and multi-platform possible later instead of impossible.

GE Healthcare four theme targets resolved from one semantic layer, so components never forked
03

Ship the smallest thing that gets used

A perfect system nobody adopts is a wiki page. I'd rather ship three components people use than thirty nobody knows about, then let real contribution friction tell me what governance actually needs to exist.

Avant marketing library became the default path because it was faster, not because it was mandated
04

Name things with the people who call them

Token and component naming is an API decision, so engineers are in the room for it. This is the single cheapest way to prevent the design-and-code drift that every system eventually gets.

Verizon design-to-code alignment through Dev Mode and Code Connect across three platforms
05

Automate the thing you've done manually three times

Not before. The third time you hand-fix the same drift is when you know the shape of the tool — and the tool you build then is the one people actually use.

Now variables → DTCG exporter, and skill files that encode the rules so generated output lands on-system
06

Measure so you know what to fix, not to prove you're winning

Coverage, detachment, adoption by team. The number matters less than the follow-up question it lets you ask.

Section 05 the nuance worth knowing about detachment rate
05

Have an opinion on these

Current practice on the two things a design systems engineer is expected to have a view about. Both give you something better than a textbook answer.

Three-tier tokens

The mature pattern — Carbon is the usual reference — is primitive → semantic → component:

primitive what it is  ·  color.blue.500 = #3B82F6
semantic what it means  ·  color.action.primary
component where it lives  ·  button.background.default
  • The rule that matters: strict one-way flow. Primitive → semantic → component, never sideways or backwards. Bidirectional references create circular dependencies that break build-time resolution.
  • Component tokens are localised exceptions, not global rules — that's the sentence that shows you understand why the tier exists rather than just that it does.
  • Your honest position: you built two tiers. Say so, say what it cost you, and say you'd add the third. That's section 03, item two.
Adoption measurement

Two complementary metrics people usually conflate:

  • Usage is breadth — which components get used, how often. Coverage is depth — what share of production UI is built from the system versus one-offs.
  • Detachment rate — how often instances get detached or overridden. And here's the nuance worth having: a high detachment rate isn't automatically bad. On a component built to be customised it may mean the component is working as designed. On a button it means your button is wrong.
  • The line to say: "a bare adoption percentage doesn't tell you which teams, or why the rest aren't. The number is only useful if it points at the next question."
  • Your qualitative one: what design review meetings are about. At Avant they shifted from policing basics to arguing about ideas.
Curiosity — have two real answers ready
  • "What are you learning right now?" Be specific and current: DTCG 2025.10 answered modes with resolvers last October, but Style Dictionary hasn't implemented them yet — the spec is ahead of the toolchain, and the runtime half is still open. Current AND honest.
  • "Where do you think design systems are headed?" Toward systems that have two consumers — humans and models — and the second one can't improvise around bad documentation. Which means documentation quality stops being a nice-to-have and becomes load-bearing.
  • Both answers end in something unresolved. That's the point — curiosity is demonstrated by naming an open question, not by expressing enthusiasm.
