01 Product judgment · PetWatch

What looked like a scheduling problem became a trust problem.

The brief described a pet-care scheduling app. A real scenario changed the framing. Hex is my cat. He takes prescribed insulin twice a day, and when I travel, a friend cares for him. My friend can remember "insulin at 7:15." What they actually need is harder to hand over: enough of my knowledge, and enough confidence, to act without me in the room.

So the product splits knowledge in two. Care Moments are time-sensitive: they become due, get done, and resolve for both people at once. Always-Know is ambient: Hex bolts through open doors, and that fact has no schedule. It matters continuously, so it never interrupts.

02 Systems thinking · Pathfinder

The problem was trust, not tidiness.

Independently reasonable decisions had accumulated across a large product. Different teams solved similar problems differently, and at scale those local decisions stopped adding up to one coherent product. The purpose of the system was never a cleaner Figma file. It was fewer repeated decisions, and more shared language.

03 Design engineering

A system written clearly enough for people can become legible to agents.

I've spent years taking design decisions out of one person's head and making them usable by someone else: another designer, an engineer, a PM. That produced tokens, components, documentation, governance. AI adds one more consumer, the agent, and the same rigor that made a system teachable to people is what makes it operable by a machine. The point is not the volume of demos below. It's that structured intent survives translation.
