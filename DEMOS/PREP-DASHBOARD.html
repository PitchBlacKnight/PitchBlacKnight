Outside the timebox, on purpose, and it says so.

Scenario
Hex (real cat, real insulin) & Jessie house-sitting. Emotionally concrete.
Approach
Domain-first. Append-only event log, derived status, 14 passing tests, then the interface. Seven-chapter case study + working prototype with a controllable clock.
What it proves
Judgment and restraint. Care Moments vs Always-Know. Snooze as agency, not failure. No MISSED state, no countdown, no red. Guided care with owner-authored stop points. NOT-BUILDING as a standing artifact. Open questions left open.
The frame shift
v1 answered "did someone handle it?" This answers "can I handle this?" A schedule transfers information; this transfers the owner's context and confidence.

Notes · the talk track

The 60-second opener

"When a brief lands, the first thing I do is feed the machine everything. The AI reads it in minutes and builds a synthesis, and my job on day one is to find the hole, the thing the documents assume but never say, because absences don't pattern-match and a model won't catch them.

From there it's a rhythm. Rank assumptions by risk, write the decision rules before the research runs, one room, one metric, one page of truth. Then build the real thing fast, because a prototype beats an opinion, and spend my hours on the part no model has: the scope cuts and the taste.

Handoff isn't a file dump. Docs generate from the same source as the artifact so they can't drift. The test of my handoff is whether they still need me in the room. They shouldn't."

Stage anecdotes (expand what you need)

Intake the hole in the brief
"On PetWatch the hole was a product with no completion state. The stories were internally consistent, nothing flagged it, and that's exactly why it's still a designer's job to find it."Pull up: petwatch-exercise.vercel.app, overview tab
Research decision rules first
"I write the decision rule before the study runs so I can't rationalize afterwards. Four of six watchers react fine, we build the active version. Three or more hate it, we ship passive. I do the interviews myself. You don't delegate listening."Pull up: validation plan in the written artifacts
The artifact the red that became amber
"First pass at overdue styling was red and urgent. Confidently produced, standard pattern, wrong product. It shouts at a volunteer doing you a favor. The amber treatment is the correction, and in the reconsidered version the whole product refuses red, and refuses a MISSED state: past-due is just due, 20 minutes ago."Pull up: reconsidered ch. 4, When life interrupts
Pressure-test snooze revealed the state model
"Snooze sounds like one small button. Implementation immediately asks: what state is the Care Moment in now? What if two watchers act? Whose timezone owns 7:15? One button revealed the whole state model, which became an event log with tests. The implementation gets to argue back."Pull up: reconsidered ch. 6, Where the interface ends
Handoff the package, not the goodbye deck
"The decision log says what we chose, what we rejected, and what would reopen each call. Docs generate from the same source as components so they physically can't drift. Tokens ship as standard DTCG. There's even a skill file so their AI tooling inherits the rules."Pull up: pbk-guide.vercel.app/pathfinder/docs

The closer (always land here)

"One honest thing, because a process that only lists successes is marketing: the AI is sometimes confidently wrong, and it matters. It produces the standard pattern with total confidence when this product needed a different one. The workflow works because a designer is standing exactly where the judgment goes. That's the job now. I think it's a better job than the one it replaced."

Questions

If they push back

Doesn't AI make designers lazy?

"It makes lazy designers faster and rigorous designers more rigorous. The edge-case sweep moved QA thinking earlier. That's more rigor, not less."

How do we know what's yours?

"I keep an AI-usage log on every project, with the failures in it. Ask me about the red overdue styling."

What if the team doesn't use AI?

"Nothing in the package requires it. Generated docs, standard tokens, a decision log. The skill file is a bonus, not a dependency."

On the v1 mistakes (lead with these before they find them)

Your data model stores ownership twice.

"Yes, and it's the exact bug class my own PRD brags about eliminating for occurrences. I'd derive owner from Membership. The reconsidered event log is that lesson, systematized."

Your privacy fallback breaks the safety case.

"The V2 'last activity' view protects the relationship by destroying the double-dosing guarantee. The fix is a third variant: passive for routine, itemized for anything safety-flagged. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary, because it's what you ship when the test goes badly."

Where did the 60% metric come from?

"Nowhere yet, honestly. They should have been labeled working hypotheses, replaced after the first five real handoffs. Same rigor as my decision rules, applied to the numbers."

Why does retention punish the product for working?

"A 90-day window on an episodic product reads a happy twice-a-year traveler as churn. Measure 'used again on the next trip' against their travel cadence instead."

Where's the emergency vet number?

"Cut by association when I excluded 'vet records' as adjacent product. Needs don't arrive in categories. Always-Know in the reconsidered version is partly that lesson."

The prototype ignores your own 5-minute undo rule.

"Right, and I called the prototype the spec. Either build the constraint or annotate exactly where the demo simplifies. The reconsidered domain model has tests for this reason."

Open questions (keep them open, that's the point)

  • Does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel monitored?
  • Does guided care actually increase first-time confidence at the moment of care?
  • Does snooze reduce anxiety or license drift?
  • Do Always-Know cards stay useful without becoming wallpaper?

If asked "did you test this?": "Not yet, and I won't pretend the design resolved these. They're ranked by cost-of-being-wrong in the research plan."
