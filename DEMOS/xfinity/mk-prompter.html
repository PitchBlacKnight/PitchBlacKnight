The number-one reason anyone opens an ISP app: "is my internet down?" So I made that the first pixel — a live status gauge. Today that answer costs about four taps. Here it costs zero.

TAP · the outage banner
Here it's one screen: live repair timeline, the map, text updates — and the part I care most about: "credit already applied." You don't ask. It just shows up on your bill. That's the single biggest trust move in the whole concept. (flip the outage back off)
SWITCH · "Viewing as" → Viewer
The app has five roles, and today tabs literally disappear for lower roles — it looks broken. My rule: roles inform, they never vanish. Everyone sees the same map; locked things are dimmed and say who holds the key. (yes — the viewer is the cat. Every account has one member like that.) (switch back to Primary)
TAP · WiFi tab → Devices → Pause something
Structural change number one: today WiFi and smart home are two separate tabs with two device lists. To a person, a camera is just another thing on the network. So WiFi absorbs Home — one device model, pause anything, one less tab.
TAP · Plan tab → Faster speeds
Structural change two: service and sales, separated. Offers live in one labeled "Explore" card — opt-in, never disguised as support. And the speed picker shows the full new price right here, taxes in — not at the end of a checkout funnel.
TAP · the ✦ button → "Internet feels slow"
Last piece: the Assistant is a layer, not a place. It answers, then deep-links you to the actual screen — watch it land on Devices. It never becomes a parallel app.

Process questions — likely asks keyword answers · every one can point back at the shared page
"Walk me through your process"
Five words: Map, Frame, Prototype, Test, Systemize. Map the territory before touching pixels — for Xfinity that was the screen map and audit. Frame it as principles — one question per tab, one address per feature. Prototype real and clickable, because stakeholders react to things they can touch. Test — and when there's no lab, audit yourself. Then systemize: the placement algorithm so the thinking outlives the project. The shared page is my process, in order.
"What does elevated craft mean to you?"
Craft is decisions you can defend — nothing on the screen by accident. A real type scale, a spacing rhythm, color as tokens not picks, motion with restraint. In the prototype: the gauge's glow is the status, not decoration; numbers are tabular so money doesn't jiggle; the hairlines catch light on one edge like a physical surface; the background moves so slowly you feel it more than see it. Craft is the last ten percent users can't name but always feel.
This is Ben's question. Slow down here. Point at actual pixels while you talk.
"How do you research without a lab?"
Evidence is everywhere before the first interview: this whole project was mapped from Comcast's own support docs, the App Store listing, and review sentiment. Support docs tell you what the company thinks the product is; reviews tell you what users experience — the gap between those two is the design brief. Then with real users I keep it lightweight: task-based tests on the five core jobs, and the Roadmap metrics decide who was right.
"How do you work with PM / Sales / Marketing?"
I'm the user's advocate who takes revenue seriously — those aren't opposites. My rule from the prototype: service and sales, separated — but sales gets a better home, not a hidden one: labeled, opt-in, full price up front. Honest offers convert better and refund less. With PMs I bring artifacts, not opinions — a tap-count table ends a debate faster than a meeting.
"How deep are you in Figma?"
Lives-and-breathes deep: variables and tokens for theming, proper variant sets, library governance so files stay one system. Pathfinder is that in public — tokens, recipes, a Figma library mapped to code. And when the workflow had a gap I built a plugin to close it — pushing tokens out to development.
"Do you use AI? How?"
Yes — like a team I direct, not a vending machine. It accelerates the production; the taste, judgment, and structure are mine — the IA, the principles, what got cut. And I keep an AI log on my exercises showing exactly where it helped, because I'd rather show you than have you wonder. The result: studio-of-one output at team speed — which is what a fast-paced team actually buys.
"Fast pace, multiple projects?"
That's been both halves of my career: enterprise programs at BCBS and GE — many teams, hard deadlines — and studio life with parallel clients and no one to hand off to. My defenses: timeboxes, working map-first so effort lands where it matters, and prototyping in the medium so there's no throwaway deliverable stage. This entire Xfinity package was one focused sprint.
"How do you take critique?"
As data. I ran my own audit against my own design — found six real issues, fixed all six, published the list. If I'll do that to myself unprompted, you can imagine crit is easy. The only thing I push back on is feedback with no user in it — and even then, I'd rather test it than argue it.
