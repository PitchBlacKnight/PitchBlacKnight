Intro 5 min · tell me about yourself
Intro

Read it straight through

"So, the short version: I make complicated things understandable. That's been the whole career. 23 years of it.

And it's funny, the industries kept changing on me. Google, GE Healthcare, Blue Cross, TransUnion, Avant. But the problem never did. Every time, it was a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. And every time, I ended up owning the design system. Not because I love component libraries, honestly. It's because the interesting problem was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant, that was Pathfinder. Two and a half years, end to end, and it's carrying 350-plus contributors in one shared language.

Then a couple of years ago, the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of what I do. And I figured I'd rather be early and hands-on than learn it secondhand. So these days I don't just design systems, I build them. And everything you're about to see actually runs.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's basically my job description."

Exercise · Prototype tab: the task list, tap a checkmark, then the Activity log

Present

4 · The second pass, live 10-16

Reconsidered prototype · advance the clock, snooze the insulin, open guided care, finish

"Quick framing before I show this: the four-hour submission you reviewed is my complete answer to the exercise. What you're about to see happened after I hit send, on my own time, because the problem kept bugging me. Your brief says you want to see how I'd shape this product's future direction. This is that, built. So… Hex is my actual cat. He gets insulin twice a day, and when I travel, a friend takes care of him. Now, my friend can remember '7:15.' Sure. But remembering it and feeling able to do it are two different things. A schedule transfers information. This thing has to transfer confidence.

So there are two kinds of knowing in here now. Care Moments have a time. They come due, they get done, and they resolve for both people at once. And Always-Know, that's the RUNNER card, has no time at all. Hex bolts through doors. That's always true. So it never interrupts, and it never goes away.

Watch what snooze does here. It's not failure. It's 'I saw this, don't let me forget.' Guided care is the owner's own words, step by step, including the stop point: 'if he hasn't eaten, stop and text Mikel.' And when you finish… nothing celebrates. It just says all taken care of. Relief, twice over. I handled it. Someone handled it.

"And the refusals are the part I'm proudest of. No countdown. No MISSED state. No red. No confetti. And no AI talking at the moment of care. Those are decisions, not omissions."

5 · Your team, through launch 16-20

Reconsidered · chapter 6, the state diagram

"Now the part you actually asked about, which is how this works with your team. So, snooze looks like one little button, right? But the second you implement it, it starts asking questions. What state is this moment in now? What happens when two watchers act at once? Whose timezone owns 7:15? So what engineering gets from me isn't a folder of screens. It's a state model backed by an event log, with passing tests. And my favorite rule in there came out of a failing test: the schedule belongs to the animal. The household's time, never the viewer's.

QA gets the edge cases during design, not in week six. Each one turns into an acceptance criterion before anything gets built. Product gets one page of truth plus a decision log in Notion. The backlog's Linear-ready. The tokens are in Figma. And everything the AI did is logged in Claude, failures included."

Exercise · Maps tab: invite lifecycle state machine

Projects

"A design system at scale" → Pathfinder

mikelrosenthal.com · Pathfinder case file: the audit board, then the config screen

"So, Avant. Two and a half years, end to end. When I got there, separate teams had layered five primary blues and three button shapes into production. And the problem wasn't tidiness. It was trust. Advisors present live revenue numbers out of this tool, and inconsistency reads as unreliability. The audit made the fragmentation countable, and countable made it fundable. It carries 350-plus contributors now, and 2.0 to 3.0 shipped on the same foundations. No rebuild.

"Best learning in the whole project: the Configuration table gave the fit score and the provider logo equal weight. And advisors just… picked the logo they recognized. Which is exactly what that screen existed to prevent. Testing broke my hierarchy, so the hierarchy changed. Same loop as PetWatch's amber."

Projects

"Non-designers? Marketing?" → Avant Marketing Library

"Same architecture, different user. 350-plus marketers, and they need harder rails and warmer documentation than designers do. One template logic ended up producing 174-plus campaign variants. And my favorite outcome: the flagship 40-page report got distilled back into templates, so the next report started at eighty percent instead of zero.

"A marketing library is just a design system with a different user. Same architecture, different empathy. And on PetWatch, the watcher is the different user."

Projects

"Regulated? Healthcare?" → FIBER and Edison

"Blue Cross FIBER was their first unified system. Forty-plus enrollment screens, web and mobile from one foundation. And accessibility got baked into the foundation layer, not bolted on afterward. Because in healthcare, accessibility decides whether the product works at all.

"Then GE's Edison. Forty-plus regulated products, and the system work won a Red Dot and a Fast Company award. The lesson from both: big systems are social systems. The kit was half the job. Adoption was the product."

Projects

"Data-heavy? Consumer trust?" → PRISM and TrueCredit

"PRISM is psychometric data. The brief was basically: let a manager act on this in thirty seconds, without anyone feeling reduced to a chart. I treated the visualization like component anatomy. Define the grammar, and the data speaks.

"And TrueCredit, honestly, is PetWatch's emotion. A number people are afraid of. We made an 890-point score legible at a glance. People flinch at credit data, and plain hierarchy is what lets them believe it. Swap 'credit score' for 'insulin at 7:15' and it's the same job: lower the temperature."

Projects

"Brand? Visual range?" → Spacelab and Maven Wave

"Spacelab went from naming all the way to packaging. Twenty-plus name candidates, and every single one got dressed in real typography before the vote, because you can't judge a name naked. Four package formats made it to production. And Maven Wave: four complete brand directions, and the deck opened by teaching the client how to judge a logo before showing them one. That identity carried them to a Google Cloud premier partnership, and eventually an acquisition.

"Same discipline as product: explore wide, decide narrow, finish hard."
