Intro 5 min · tell me about yourself
Intro

Read it straight through

"So, the short version: I make complicated things understandable. That's been the whole career. 23 years of it.

The industries kept changing on me. Google, GE Healthcare, Blue Cross, TransUnion, Avant. But the problem never did. Every time, it was a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. And every time, I ended up owning the design system. Because the interesting problem was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant, that was Pathfinder. Two and a half years, end to end, 350-plus contributors in one shared language.

Then the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of what I do, and I decided I'd rather be early and hands-on than learn it secondhand. So now I don't just design systems, I build them. And everything you're about to see actually runs.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's basically my job description."

Present 25 min · 7 beats
Present

1 · What the brief asked 0-3

Demo 1 · Overview tab

"So the brief describes a care app. Pets, routines, invites. And there's one really smart modeling decision in there: owner and watcher aren't roles, they're a relationship to a particular pet. I kept that at the center of everything.

The brief also says it's intentionally vague, and it asks for assumptions out loud. So that's how I worked. Every assumption is labeled. And there are two places where I pushed back on a user story on purpose. I'll flag both."

Present

2 · How I spent the four hours 3-7

Demo 1 · Documents tab, then Maps tab: the 20-screen inventory

"Hour one, I didn't sketch anything. I read everything and hunted for what was missing. Then a PRD with a Not-Building table. A backlog your TPMs could drop straight into Linear. A research plan where the decision rules got written before the studies run. And this inventory: twenty screens, each one marked prototyped, specified, or deferred.

"You asked how I prioritize work I don't do. That deferred column is the answer. Every cut is written down, with a reason."

Present

3 · The hole 7-10

Demo 1 · Prototype tab: tap a checkmark, then the Activity log

"Here's what day one found. The stories describe a schedule that gets written by an owner and read by a watcher. And that's it. Nobody can ever mark anything done. This checkmark, this activity log… none of it exists in the brief. And the stories are internally consistent, so nothing flags it. That's why finding it is still a designer's job. Absences don't pattern-match.

And once completion exists, the stakes show up. Two of the sample tasks are medication. No completion state, and the failure mode is a double-dosed animal. That's when this stopped being a calendar and became a trust product."

Present

4 · The second pass, live 10-16

Demo 2 prototype · advance the clock, snooze the insulin, open guided care, finish

"Quick framing first: the four-hour submission you reviewed is my complete answer to the exercise. What I'm about to show happened after I hit send, on my own time, because the problem kept bugging me. You said you want to see how I'd shape this product's future direction. This is that, built.

So. Hex is my actual cat. Insulin twice a day, and when I travel, a friend takes care of him. My friend can remember '7:15.' But remembering it and feeling able to do it are two different things. A schedule transfers information. This has to transfer confidence.

Two kinds of knowing now. Care Moments have a time: they come due, get done, and resolve for both people at once. Always-Know, the RUNNER card, has no time: Hex bolts through doors, that's always true, so it never interrupts and never goes away. Snooze isn't failure, it's 'I saw this, don't let me forget.' Guided care is the owner's own words, including the stop point: 'if he hasn't eaten, stop and text Mikel.' And when you finish, nothing celebrates. It just says all taken care of. Relief, twice. I handled it. Someone handled it.

"The refusals are the part I'm proudest of. No countdown. No MISSED state. No red. No confetti. No AI talking at the moment of care. Those are decisions, not omissions."

Present

5 · Your team, through launch 16-20

Demo 2 · chapter 6, the state diagram

"Now, how this works with your team. Snooze looks like one little button, right? But the second you build it, it starts asking questions. What state is this moment in now? What if two watchers act at once? Whose timezone owns 7:15? So engineering doesn't get a folder of screens from me. They get a state model backed by an event log, with passing tests. My favorite rule in there came from a failing test: the schedule belongs to the animal. The household's time, never the viewer's.

QA gets the edge cases during design, not week six, and each one becomes an acceptance criterion before build. Product gets one page of truth and a decision log in Notion. The backlog is Linear-ready. Tokens live in Figma. And the AI work is logged in Claude, failures included."

Present

6 · Two pushbacks I owe you 20-22

Demo 1 · Maps tab: invite lifecycle state machine

"Two places I deliberately pushed back, both flagged as assumptions. The weekly view: for a watcher, the real frame isn't a calendar week, it's the stay. Day one of five. The owner writing the routine still needs weekly editing, so that view lives with the owner: deferred, not deleted. And the invite rule, registered users only: instead of faking a signup flow the constraint doesn't allow, the app just tells the truth and hands the owner a pre-written message to send. Honest about the constraint… and honest that it puts work on the busiest person in the story. That's the first thing I'd test."

Present

7 · AI honestly, and the close 22-25

"You encouraged AI use, so here's the honest ledger. It compressed the reading, the first drafts, the edge-case lists, and the build itself. That's how four hours produced this much. And it was also confidently wrong, three times: a ticking countdown, a MISSED state, and copy that read like a compliance memo. Standard patterns. Wrong product. All three rejections are written down.

"So the workflow works because a designer is standing exactly where the judgment goes. Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's the talk. Questions?"

Their Q&A

"Where's the weekly view we asked for?"

Demo 1 · Maps: the Weekly row, marked deferred · or Demo 2 prototype: "Day 1 of 5"

"I built the need, not the artifact. A watcher's real question is 'what's next, and can I handle it?' The stay answers that better than a week grid. The owner still needs weekly editing to write the routine, so that view lives with the owner: deferred, not deleted. And if research showed watchers planning their weeks around tasks? That changes my mind. That trigger's written down."

Their Q&A

"Walk us through the invite flow."

Demo 1 · Prototype: Invite a watcher → Invitation sent → "You're watching Kenji" → Remove access?

"The constraint says invitees already have accounts. So instead of faking onboarding, the app tells the truth: your friend needs an account first, and here's a pre-written message to send them. Revoke is first-class, because the brief promises owners visibility and control. The whole lifecycle is a state machine: invited, accepted, revoked, expired.

"And I'll volunteer the weak spot: this puts work on an anxious owner who's busy packing. First flow I'd test."

Their Q&A

"How would you validate before building?"

Demo 1 · Documents: research plan, the decision rules

"Assumptions get ranked by what it costs to be wrong. And the decision rules get written before the study runs, so I can't rationalize afterward. Top of the list: does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel watched? Four of six watchers fine with it, we ship itemized. Three or more not, we ship passive.

"And I do the interviews myself. You don't delegate listening."

Their Q&A

"What ships first?"

Demo 1 · Maps: the 20 screens, point at the prototyped column

"The smallest thing that completes the trust loop. One pet, one watcher, care moments with completion, the activity view. The gate is five real handoffs, real people, real pets. Everything else waits in line behind that, and the Not-Building table says why."

Their Q&A

"Where did your metrics come from?"

Demo 1 · Documents: PRD metrics table, point right at it

"Honestly? Nowhere. That's the flaw in this table. The 60 percent looks measured and it isn't. It should've said working hypotheses, replace after five real handoffs. And the retention number actually punishes the product for working: a 90-day window on a product people use twice a year reads a happy customer as churn. I'd measure 'used again on the next trip' instead."

Their Q&A

"How do you work with PMs?"

"The PM owns the why and the priority. I own making the decision legible. The artifact I care most about is the one page of truth from alignment: one metric, the v1 cut line, the riskiest assumption, an owner for each. If a decision isn't on that page, it didn't happen. Strategy before the build is how you all work too. That page is what a strategy sprint should leave behind."

Their Q&A

"How do you hand off to engineers?"

Demo 2 · chapter 6: the event log and tests

"As a system, not a folder of pictures. The state model, the event log, tokens in standard formats, and a decision log: what we chose, what we rejected, and what would reopen each call.

"The test is simple: do they still need me in the room? They shouldn't."

Their Q&A

"How do you work with QA?"

Demo 2 · chapter 6 state diagram, the orbiting edge cases

"The edge cases get listed during design, not discovered in week six. Two watchers at once. Offline at the moment of care. Stale client. Revoked access mid-stay. Duplicate completion. Each becomes an acceptance criterion before build. An edge case is usually just a product decision we haven't made yet. I'd rather make it on purpose."

Their Q&A

"Doesn't AI make designers lazy?"

"It makes lazy designers faster, and rigorous designers more rigorous. The edge-case sweep moved QA thinking into the design phase. That's more rigor, not less."

Demo 2 prototype: point at the amber past-due task, no red anywhere

"Yeah. First pass at the overdue state came back red and urgent. Totally confident, totally standard… and wrong for this product. Red shouts. And you'd be shouting at a volunteer doing you a favor. Amber is the correction, and the second pass refuses red entirely.

Their Q&A

"Why redo it? Isn't that outside the timebox?"

"Because the timed version answered 'did someone handle it?' and never answered 'can I handle this?' That second question is the real product. The original is still public, untouched. Four hours, unedited. And the second version says on its face that it broke the timebox, and why.

"Reconsidering your own shipped answer… that is product judgment."

Their Q&A

"Is the second version even what we asked for?"

"Fair challenge. On its own? No. It skips auth, invites, most of the surface. That's why it's not my submission. The four-hour version answers the ask: twenty screens dispositioned, the documents, the prototype, inside the timebox.

But you said you're judging how I'd shape this product's future direction. The second pass is what my thinking does when nobody's paying for it. On a real project, that's the weeks after launch: ship the scoped thing on time, then let the evidence reopen decisions.

"The four hours show what I deliver inside a constraint. The second pass shows what you get living with me on the project."

Their Q&A

"What's wrong with your v1?" (own each fast)

Demo 1 · Maps: the object map, point at the two ownership edges

Ownership stored twice: "See these two edges? Both say who owns the pet. And the brief itself says roles are per-pet. My Membership table was the right instinct… the stray owner field betrayed it. I'd derive ownership. The event log in the second pass is that lesson, systematized."

Privacy fallback vs. safety: "My passive fallback protected the relationship by quietly destroying the double-dosing guarantee. The fix is a third variant, itemized for anything safety-flagged. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary."

Missing emergency vet number: "Cut by association. I excluded 'vet records,' and the emergency number went with it. Needs don't arrive in categories. Always-Know exists partly because of that."

The visual seams (say it before they see it): "And the craft has seams. Count the oranges on this screen: five, and none of them reconcile. The hero stats say two done of seven, one waiting, five more… that's eight. Four hours buys a direction, not a reconciled system. That's why the second pass runs on one accent in one token file, and why QA moved upstream in my process. I left it all exactly as I shipped it. An honest baseline is worth more than a quietly polished one."

Other projects when they pivot
Projects

"A design system at scale" → Pathfinder

mikelrosenthal.com · Pathfinder case file: the audit board, then the config screen

"Avant. Two and a half years, end to end. When I got there, separate teams had layered five primary blues and three button shapes into production. The problem wasn't tidiness, it was trust: advisors present live revenue numbers out of this tool, and inconsistency reads as unreliability. The audit made the fragmentation countable, and countable made it fundable. It carries 350-plus contributors now, and 2.0 to 3.0 shipped on the same foundations. No rebuild.

"Best learning: the Configuration table gave the fit score and the provider logo equal weight, and advisors just… picked the logo they recognized. Exactly what the screen existed to prevent. Testing broke my hierarchy, so the hierarchy changed. Same loop as PetWatch's amber."

Projects

"Non-designers? Marketing?" → Avant Marketing Library

"Same architecture, different user. 350-plus marketers, who need harder rails and warmer documentation than designers. One template logic produced 174-plus campaign variants, and the flagship 40-page report got distilled back into templates, so the next report started at eighty percent instead of zero.

"A marketing library is a design system with a different user. Same architecture, different empathy. On PetWatch, the watcher is the different user."

Projects

"Regulated? Healthcare?" → FIBER and Edison

"Blue Cross FIBER was their first unified system: forty-plus enrollment screens, web and mobile, one foundation. Accessibility baked into the foundation layer, because in healthcare, accessibility decides whether the product works at all.

"GE's Edison: forty-plus regulated products, a Red Dot and a Fast Company award. The lesson from both: big systems are social systems. The kit was half the job. Adoption was the product."

Projects

"Data-heavy? Consumer trust?" → PRISM and TrueCredit

"PRISM is psychometric data: let a manager act on it in thirty seconds without anyone feeling reduced to a chart. I treated visualization like component anatomy: define the grammar, and the data speaks.

"TrueCredit is PetWatch's emotion: a number people are afraid of. An 890-point score, legible at a glance. People flinch at credit data, and plain hierarchy lets them believe it. Swap 'credit score' for 'insulin at 7:15': same job. Lower the temperature."

Projects

"Brand? Visual range?" → Spacelab and Maven Wave

"Spacelab: naming through packaging. Twenty-plus name candidates, each dressed in real typography before the vote, because you can't judge a name naked. Four package formats in production. Maven Wave: four complete brand directions, and the deck opened by teaching the client how to judge a logo before showing one. That identity carried them to a Google Cloud premier partnership and an acquisition.

"Same discipline as product: explore wide, decide narrow, finish hard."
