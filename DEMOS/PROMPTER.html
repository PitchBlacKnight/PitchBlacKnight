00 · Tell me about yourself (75 seconds)

"The short version: I make complicated systems understandable, and I've been doing it for 23 years.

The long version has a pattern. Blue Cross, GE Healthcare, TransUnion, Avant. Different industries, but every team had the same problem: a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. And on every one of those teams I ended up owning the design system, because the interesting problem to me was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant that was Pathfinder: two and a half years, end to end, 350-plus contributors working in one shared language.

Then the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of my whole discipline, and I decided I'd rather be early and hands-on than fluent in it secondhand. So today I don't just design systems, I build them: a design system that runs as live code, an agent that assembles working interfaces from it, and product work where the prototype has a tested domain model behind it. All of it is real, and I can show you any of it in the next half hour.

Why I'm in this room: the thing I've always cared about, judgment surviving scale, just became the scarcest thing in the process.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's my job description."

01 · "Walk me through how you work" (60 seconds)

"When a brief lands, the first thing I do is feed the machine everything. The brief, the old research, the emails nobody rereads. The AI reads it in minutes and builds me a synthesis, and my job on day one is to find the hole, the thing the documents assume but never say, because absences don't pattern-match and a model won't catch them.

From there it's a rhythm. I rank the assumptions by risk and write the decision rules before the research runs. One room, one metric, one page of truth. Then we build the real thing fast, because a prototype beats an opinion, and I spend my hours on the part no model has: the scope cuts and the taste.

And handoff isn't a file dump. The docs generate from the same source as the artifact so they can't drift. The test of my handoff is whether they still need me in the room. They shouldn't."

02 · The PetWatch story, four beats

Beat 1 the hole in the brief

"The brief looked like a scheduling app, and the user stories were internally consistent. But the schedule they described gets written and read, and never done. Nobody could ever mark anything complete. Nothing in the documents flagged that, and that's exactly why it's still a designer's job to find it."

Beat 2 Hex

"Hex is my actual cat. He takes prescribed insulin twice a day, and when I travel, a friend takes care of him. My friend can remember 'insulin at 7:15.' That doesn't mean they feel able to do it. What they're actually thinking is: this is my friend's animal, and I don't want to hurt him.

So the real product question changed. A schedule transfers information. This product has to transfer confidence."

Beat 3 two kinds of knowing

"That split the product in two. Care Moments are time-sensitive: insulin at 7:15 becomes due, gets done, and resolves for both people at once. Always-Know is different: Hex bolts through open doors. That fact has no schedule. It matters continuously, so it never interrupts and never goes away.

Some knowledge has a time. Some knowledge has a door."

Beat 4 the refusals

"The decisions I'm proudest of are refusals. There's no countdown, because a countdown counts down to stress. There's no MISSED state, past-due is just 'due, twenty minutes ago,' because verdicts belong to the owner, not the system. There's no red anywhere, because red shouts at a volunteer doing you a favor. There's no confetti, completion is relief, twice over: I handled it, and someone handled it. And no AI speaks at the moment of care, every instruction comes from the owner and the vet, verbatim.

Every one of those is a decision, not an omission."

03 · They'll ask about AI

"Doesn't AI make designers lazy?"

"It makes lazy designers faster and rigorous designers more rigorous. The edge-case sweep moved QA thinking into the design phase. That's more rigor, not less."

"How do we know what's yours?"

"I keep an AI-usage log on every project, and the failures are in it. Ask me about the red overdue styling."

"Tell me about the red styling." (they will)

"The first pass at overdue styling was red and urgent. Confidently produced, completely standard pattern, and wrong for this product, because it shouts at a volunteer doing you a favor. The amber treatment is the correction. That's the job now: standing exactly where the judgment goes."

"What if our team doesn't use AI?"

"Nothing in the package requires it. Generated docs, standard tokens, a decision log. The skill file is a bonus, not a dependency."

04 · They'll ask about the two versions

"Why did you redo it?"

"The timed version answered 'did someone handle it?' It never answered 'can I handle this?', and that's the real product. Reconsidering your own shipped answer is part of product judgment."

"Isn't it cheating to go outside the timebox?"

"The original is still public and untouched, four hours, unedited. The second version says on its face that it broke the timebox, and why. I'd rather show you both than pretend the first one was finished thinking."

"What would you do next?"

"Run the research plan. There are four open questions, ranked by the cost of being wrong. The top one: does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel monitored? I won't pretend the design resolved those. Testing will."

05 · If they dig into v1, own it fast

The data model stores ownership twice

"Yes, and it's the exact bug class my own PRD brags about eliminating for occurrences. I'd drop the owner field and derive ownership from Membership. The reconsidered version's event log is that lesson, systematized."

The privacy fallback vs. the safety case

"My passive fallback protects the relationship by destroying the double-dosing guarantee, and I never stress-tested it. The fix is a third variant: passive for routine tasks, itemized for anything safety-flagged. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary, because it's what you ship when the test goes badly."

"Where did the 60% metric come from?"

"Nowhere yet, honestly. They should have been labeled working hypotheses, replaced after the first five real handoffs. Same rigor as my decision rules, applied to the numbers."

The retention metric

"A 90-day window on an episodic product reads a happy twice-a-year traveler as churn. I'd measure 'used again on the next trip' against their actual travel cadence instead."

The missing emergency vet number

"It got cut by association when I excluded vet records as adjacent product. Needs don't arrive in categories. Now when I cut a category, I ask which single field inside it was doing the real work. Always-Know is partly that lesson."

"One honest thing, because a process that only lists successes is marketing: the AI is sometimes confidently wrong, and it matters. It produces the standard pattern with total confidence when this product needed a different one. The workflow works because a designer is standing exactly where the judgment goes.

That's the job now. I think it's a better job than the one it replaced."
