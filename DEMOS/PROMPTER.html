Intros · 5 min

"The short version: I make complicated systems understandable, and I've been doing it for 23 years.

Google, GE Healthcare, Blue Cross, TransUnion, Avant. Different industries, same problem every time: a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. On every one of those teams I ended up owning the design system, because the interesting problem was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant that was Pathfinder: two and a half years, end to end, 350-plus contributors in one shared language.

Then the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of my discipline, and I decided I'd rather be early and hands-on than fluent in it secondhand. So now I don't just design systems, I build them, and everything you'll see in the next twenty-five minutes actually runs.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's my job description."

Present · 25 min · 7 beats

1 · What the brief asked 0-3

Exercise · Overview tab

"The brief describes pets, routines, invites. And one genuinely elegant modeling decision: owner and watcher aren't global roles, they're a relationship to a particular pet. I kept that at the center of the data model.

The exercise says it's intentionally vague and asks for assumptions out loud, so every assumption is labeled, and I'll flag the two places I deliberately pushed back on a user story."

2 · How I spent four hours 3-7

Exercise · Documents tab, then Maps tab: the 20-screen inventory

"Hour one was ingestion and the hole-hunt, not sketching. Then a PRD with a Not-Building table, a backlog with IDs a TPM can drop straight into Linear, a research plan with decision rules written before the studies run, and this screen inventory: all twenty screens marked prototyped, specified, or deferred.

"You asked how I prioritize work I don't do. The deferred column is the answer: every cut is written down, with a reason."

3 · The hole 7-10

Exercise · Prototype tab: the task list, tap a checkmark, then the Activity log

"Here's what day one found. The stories describe a schedule that gets written by an owner and read by a watcher. Nobody can ever mark anything done. This checkmark, this activity log, none of it exists in the brief. The routine is internally consistent, nothing flags it, and that's why finding it is still a designer's job: absences don't pattern-match.

And once completion exists, the stakes appear: two sample tasks are medication. Without completion state, the failure mode is a double-dosed animal. That turns a calendar into a trust product."

4 · The second pass, live 10-16

Reconsidered prototype · advance the clock, snooze the insulin, open guided care, finish

"The problem kept bothering me after I submitted, so I did it twice. Hex is my actual cat, insulin twice a day. My friend can remember '7:15.' That doesn't mean they feel able to do it. A schedule transfers information. This has to transfer confidence.

Two kinds of knowing: Care Moments become due and resolve for both people at once. Always-Know, the RUNNER card, has no schedule, so it never interrupts and never goes away. Snooze means 'I saw this, don't let me forget,' not failure. Guided care carries the owner's own words, including the stop point: 'if he hasn't eaten, stop and text Mikel.' And completion is quiet. I handled it. Someone handled it.

"The refusals are deliberate: no countdown, no MISSED state, no red, no confetti, no AI speaking at the moment of care. Decisions, not omissions."

5 · Your team, through launch 16-20

Reconsidered · chapter 6, the state diagram

"Snooze looks like a button, but implementation immediately asks: what state is the moment in now? What if two watchers act at once? Whose timezone owns 7:15? So engineering gets a state model backed by an event log with passing tests, not a folder of screens. The rule a failing test produced: the schedule belongs to the animal, the household's time, never the viewer's.

QA gets edge cases enumerated during design, not week six, each one an acceptance criterion before build. Product gets one page of truth and a decision log in Notion. The backlog is Linear-ready. Tokens live in Figma. The AI work is logged in Claude, failures included."

6 · Two pushbacks I owe you 20-22

Exercise · Maps tab: invite lifecycle state machine

"Two deliberate deviations, flagged as assumptions. The weekly view: a caregiver's real frame is the stay, day one of five, not a calendar week. The owner authoring a routine still needs weekly editing, so it lives with the owner, deferred, not deleted. And the registered-users-only invite: rather than fake a flow the constraint forbids, the product refuses gracefully and hands the owner a pre-written message. Honest about the constraint, and honest that it puts work on the busiest person, which is the first thing I'd test."

7 · AI honestly, and the close 22-25

"You encouraged AI use, so here's the ledger. It compressed ingestion, drafts, edge-case enumeration, and the build itself: that's how four hours produced this much. And it was confidently wrong three times: a ticking countdown, a MISSED state, compliance-toned copy. Standard patterns, wrong product. All three rejections are documented.

"The workflow works because a designer stands exactly where the judgment goes. Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. Questions."

Exercise · Prototype: Invite a watcher → Invitation sent → "You're watching Kenji" → Remove access?

"The constraint says invitees must already have accounts. "How would you validate before building?"

Exercise · Documents: research plan, the decision rules

"Assumptions ranked by cost of being wrong, and the decision rules written before the study runs so I can't rationalize afterward. Top of the list: does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel monitored? Four of six watchers react fine, we ship itemized; three or more don't, we ship passive.

"I do the interviews myself. You don't delegate listening."

"The PM owns the why and the priority; I own making the decision legible. "Why redo it? Isn't that outside the timebox?"

"The timed version answered 'did someone handle it?' It never answered 'can I handle this?', and that's the real product. The original is still public, untouched, four hours, unedited, and the second version says on its face that it broke the timebox and why.

"Reconsidering your own shipped answer is part of product judgment."

"What's wrong with your v1?" (own each fast)

Exercise · Maps: the object map, point at the two ownership edges

Ownership stored twice: "This map has two edges saying who owns the pet, and the brief itself says roles are per-pet. My Membership table was the right instinct; the stray owner field betrayed it. I'd derive ownership. The event log is that lesson, systematized."

Privacy fallback vs. safety: "My passive fallback protected the relationship by destroying the double-dosing guarantee. Fix: a third variant, itemized for safety-flagged tasks. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary."

Missing emergency vet field: "Cut by association with 'vet records.' Needs don't arrive in categories. Always-Know is partly that lesson."
