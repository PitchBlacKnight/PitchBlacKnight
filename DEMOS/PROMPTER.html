Intros · 5 min · Tell me about yourself

"The short version: I make complicated systems understandable, and I've been doing it for 23 years.

The long version has a pattern. Google, GE Healthcare, Blue Cross, TransUnion, Avant. Different industries, but every team had the same problem: a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. On every one of those teams I ended up owning the design system, because the interesting problem was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant that was Pathfinder: two and a half years, end to end, 350-plus contributors working in one shared language.

Then the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of my discipline, and I decided I'd rather be early and hands-on than fluent in it secondhand. So today I don't just design systems, I build them: a design system that runs as live code, an agent that assembles working interfaces from it, and this exercise, where the prototype has a tested domain model behind it. All of it is real, and you'll see it in the next twenty-five minutes.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's my job description."

Presentation · 25 min · seven beats

Beat 1 · What the brief asked 0-3

petwatch-exercise.vercel.app, Overview tab

"The brief describes a care app: pets, routines, invites. And it makes one genuinely elegant modeling decision: owner and watcher aren't global roles, they're a relationship to a particular pet. I kept that at the center of the data model.

The exercise says it's intentionally vague and asks for assumptions out loud, so that's how I worked: every assumption is labeled in the submission, and I'll flag the two places where I deliberately pushed back on a user story as we go."

Beat 2 · How I spent the four hours 3-7

Exercise tabs: Documents, Maps

"Structure first. Hour one was ingestion and the hole-hunt, not sketching. Then a PRD with a Not-Building table, a backlog with IDs a TPM could drop into Linear, a research plan with decision rules written before the studies run, flows and a screen inventory where all twenty screens are marked prototyped, specified, or deferred, and a working prototype.

"The deferred column is on purpose. You asked how I prioritize work I don't do, and that column is the answer: every cut is written down, with a reason."

Beat 3 · The hole 7-10

"Here's what day one found. The stories describe a schedule that gets written by an owner and read by a watcher. Nobody can ever mark anything done. The routine is internally consistent, nothing flags it, and that's exactly why finding it is still a designer's job: absences don't pattern-match.

And once completion exists, the stakes appear: two of the sample tasks are medication. Without completion state, the failure mode is a double-dosed animal. That turned this from a calendar into a trust product."

Beat 4 · The second pass, live 10-16

petwatch-reconsidered.vercel.app/prototype.html · use the clock

"After I submitted the timed version, the problem kept bothering me, so I did it twice. Hex is my actual cat, insulin twice a day. My friend can remember '7:15.' That doesn't mean they feel able to do it. A schedule transfers information. This has to transfer confidence.

Two kinds of knowing: Care Moments become due and resolve for both people at once. Always-Know, like 'Hex bolts through doors,' has no schedule, so it never interrupts and never goes away. Watch the clock: snooze means 'I saw this, don't let me forget,' not failure. Guided care carries the owner's own words, including the stop point: 'if he hasn't eaten, stop and text Mikel.' And completion is quiet. Relief, twice over: I handled it. Someone handled it."

"The refusals are deliberate: no countdown, no MISSED state, no red, no confetti, and no AI speaking at the moment of care. Every one is a decision, not an omission."

Beat 5 · The team through launch 16-20

Reconsidered, chapter 6: Where the interface ends

"Now the part you actually asked about: how this works with your team. Snooze looks like a button, but implementation immediately asks: what state is the moment in now? What if two watchers act at once? Whose timezone owns 7:15? So the deliverable to engineering isn't screens, it's a state model backed by an event log with passing tests. The rule that came out of a failing test: the schedule belongs to the animal, so moments anchor to the household's time, never the viewer's.

For QA, edge cases get enumerated during design, not week six: offline at the moment of care, stale client, revoked access, duplicate completion. Each becomes an acceptance criterion before the build starts. For product, there's one page of truth and a decision log in Notion; the backlog IDs are Linear-ready; tokens and specs live in Figma; and the AI work is logged in Claude, failures included."

Beat 6 · The two pushbacks I owe you 20-22

"Two places I deliberately deviated from the stories, flagged as assumptions. First, the weekly view. For the watcher I reframed it: a caregiver's real frame is the stay, day one of five, not a calendar week. The owner authoring a routine still needs week-shaped editing, so that view lives with the owner, and it's in the deferred column, not deleted. Second, the registered-users-only invite. Rather than fake a flow the constraint forbids, the product refuses gracefully and hands the owner a pre-written message to send. Honest about the constraint, and honest in the submission that this puts work on the busiest person, which is the first thing I'd test."

Beat 7 · AI, honestly, and the close 22-25

"You encouraged AI use, so here's the honest ledger. It compressed ingestion, first drafts, edge-case enumeration, and the build itself; that's how a four-hour window produced this much. And it was confidently wrong three times: it proposed a ticking countdown, a MISSED state, and compliance-toned copy. Standard patterns, wrong product. All three rejections are documented.

"So the workflow works because a designer stands exactly where the judgment goes. Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. Happy to take questions."

Their Q&A · 15 min

"Why didn't you build the weekly view we asked for?"

"I built the need, not the artifact. The watcher's real question is 'what's next, and can I handle it', which the stay view answers better than a week grid. The owner's authoring flow still needs weekly editing, so it's specified and deferred, not deleted. If watcher research showed people planning their own weeks around tasks, that would change my mind, and that trigger is written in the submission."

"How would you validate this before we build it?"

"The research plan ranks assumptions by cost of being wrong, and the decision rules are written before the study runs so I can't rationalize afterward. Top of the list: does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel monitored? Four of six watchers react fine, we ship the itemized view; three or more don't, we ship passive. I do the interviews myself. You don't delegate listening."

"What ships first? Walk us through the release."

"Release one is the smallest thing that completes the trust loop: one pet, one watcher, care moments with completion, and the activity view. The gate is five real handoffs with real pets. Invites-at-scale, multi-watcher UI, notifications infrastructure, all sequenced behind it, and the Not-Building table says why each one waits."

"How do you work with PMs? Who decides?"

"The PM owns the why and the priority; I own making the decision legible. My favorite artifact is the one page of truth from the alignment session: one metric, the v1 cut line, the riskiest assumption, an owner for each. If a decision isn't on that page, it didn't happen. Strategy first is how you work too; that page is what a strategy sprint should leave behind."

"How do you hand off to engineers?"

"As a system, not a folder of pictures: the state model and event log, tokens in standard formats, and a decision log that says what we chose, what we rejected, and what would reopen each call. The test of my handoff is whether they still need me in the room. They shouldn't."

"Doesn't AI make designers lazy?"

"It makes lazy designers faster and rigorous designers more rigorous. The edge-case sweep moved QA thinking into the design phase. That's more rigor, not less."

"How do we know what's yours?"

"I keep an AI-usage log on every project, and the failures are in it. Ask me about the red overdue styling."

"Tell me about the red styling." (they will)

"First pass at overdue was red and urgent. Confidently produced, standard pattern, wrong product: it shouts at a volunteer doing you a favor. Amber is the correction. That's the job: standing exactly where the judgment goes."

"Why redo it? Isn't that outside the timebox?"

"The timed version answered 'did someone handle it?' It never answered 'can I handle this?', and that's the real product. The original is still public, untouched, and the second version says on its face that it broke the timebox and why. Reconsidering your own shipped answer is part of product judgment."

If they dig into v1 flaws (own each fast)

Ownership stored twice: "Yes, and the brief itself says roles are per-pet. My Membership table was the right instinct; the stray owner field betrayed it. I'd derive ownership. The event log is that lesson, systematized."

Privacy fallback vs. safety: "My passive fallback protected the relationship by destroying the double-dosing guarantee. Fix: a third variant, itemized for anything safety-flagged. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary."

The 60% metric: "Honestly, nowhere yet. Should have been labeled working hypotheses, replaced after five real handoffs."

Missing emergency vet field: "Cut by association with 'vet records.' Needs don't arrive in categories. Always-Know is partly that lesson."

Backup stories · when they ask about specifics

"Tell us about a design system at scale" → Pathfinder

"Avant, two and a half years, end to end. Separate teams had layered five primary blues and three button shapes into production; the problem was trust, not tidiness. I made the fragmentation countable with an audit, which doubled as the funding argument. The system now carries 350-plus contributors, and version 2 to 3 shipped on the same foundations, no rebuild.

"Best moment of learning: the Configuration table gave the fit score and the provider logo equal weight, and advisors chose by brand recognition, exactly what the screen existed to prevent. Testing broke my hierarchy, so the hierarchy changed. Same loop as PetWatch's amber."

"What about non-designers / marketing?" → Avant Marketing Library

"After Pathfinder, marketing was the next bottleneck. Same architecture, different user: 350-plus marketers who need harder rails and warmer documentation than designers do. One template logic produced 174-plus campaign variants, and the flagship 40-page industry report got distilled back into templates, so the next report started at eighty percent instead of zero.

"A marketing library is a design system with a different user. The architecture is the same; the empathy is different. That's also my answer for PetWatch: the watcher is the different user."

"Regulated / healthcare?" → FIBER and Edison

"Blue Cross FIBER was their first unified system: forty-plus enrollment screens, web and mobile from one foundation, and accessibility baked into the foundation layer, because in healthcare accessibility decides whether the product works at all. I presented the guide personally: not a spec dump, an argument.

"GE Edison unified forty-plus regulated products across four theme targets and won a Red Dot and a Fast Company award in 2020. The lesson I carry: big systems are social systems. The hardest problems were alignment, not visuals. The kit was half the job; adoption was the product."

"Data-heavy / consumer trust?" → PRISM and TrueCredit

"PRISM is psychometric data: the brief was to let a manager act on it in thirty seconds without making anyone feel reduced to a chart. I treated visualization as component anatomy: define the grammar and the data speaks.

"TrueCredit is the same emotion PetWatch has: a number people are afraid of. An 890-point score made legible at a glance, five full visual directions before choosing. People flinch at credit data. Plain hierarchy is what lets them believe it. Swap 'credit score' for 'insulin at 7:15' and it's the same design problem: lower the temperature."

"Brand / visual range?" → Spacelab and Maven Wave

"Spacelab: naming through packaging, twenty-plus names each dressed in real typography before the vote, a 38-page deck, four package formats in production. Maven Wave: four complete brand directions, and the deck opened by teaching the client how to judge a logo before showing one; that identity carried them to a Google Cloud premier partnership and an acquisition.

"Same discipline as product: explore wide, decide narrow, finish hard."
