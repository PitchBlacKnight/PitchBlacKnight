Intro 5 min · tell me about yourself
Intro

Read it straight through

"So, the short version: I make complicated things understandable. That's been the whole career. 23 years of it.

The industries kept changing on me. Google, GE Healthcare, Blue Cross, TransUnion, Avant. But the problem never did. Every time, it was a complex product that had outgrown the decisions inside it. And every time, I ended up owning the design system. Because the interesting problem was never the screen. It was how a good decision survives contact with three hundred other people. At Avant, that was Pathfinder. Two and a half years, end to end, 350-plus contributors in one shared language.

Then the ground shifted. AI started changing the economics of what I do, and I decided I'd rather be early and hands-on than learn it secondhand. So now I don't just design systems, I build them. And everything you're about to see actually runs.

Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's basically my job description."

Present 25 min · 7 beats
Present

1 · What the brief asked 0-3

Demo 1 · Overview tab

"So the brief describes a care app. Pets, routines, invites. And there's one really smart modeling decision in there: owner and watcher aren't roles, they're a relationship to a particular pet. I kept that at the center of everything.

The brief also says it's intentionally vague, and it asks for assumptions out loud. So that's how I worked. Every assumption is labeled. And there are two places where I pushed back on a user story on purpose. I'll flag both."

Present

2 · How I spent the four hours 3-7

Demo 1 · Documents tab, then Maps tab: the 20-screen inventory

"Hour one, I didn't sketch anything. I read everything and hunted for what was missing. Then a PRD with a Not-Building table. A backlog your TPMs could drop straight into Linear. A research plan where the decision rules got written before the studies run. And this inventory: twenty screens, each one marked prototyped, specified, or deferred.

"You asked how I prioritize work I don't do. That deferred column is the answer. Every cut is written down, with a reason."

Present

3 · The hole 7-10

Demo 1 · Prototype tab: tap a checkmark, then the Activity log

"Here's what day one found. The stories describe a schedule that gets written by an owner and read by a watcher. And that's it. Nobody can ever mark anything done. This checkmark, this activity log… none of it exists in the brief. And the stories are internally consistent, so nothing flags it. That's why finding it is still a designer's job. Absences don't pattern-match.

And once completion exists, the stakes show up. Two of the sample tasks are medication. No completion state, and the failure mode is a double-dosed animal. That's when this stopped being a calendar and became a trust product."

Present

4 · The prototype, live 10-16

The exercise · Prototype tab: complete the walk, undo it, try the invite, show the activity log

"Let me just use it. Completing a task is one tap, optimistic, with undo, because a watcher mid-walk shouldn't fight a workflow. And watch the medication task: there's a double-completion guard, because two well-meaning people is the scary scenario. The failure mode there is a double-dosed animal, so that guard is the most important interaction in the product.

The invite flow: the brief says invitees already have accounts. So instead of faking a signup, the app tells the truth and hands Priya a pre-written text to send herself. And revoke is first-class, because the brief promises owners visibility and control.

Two small things I want you to notice. Waiting is amber, never red. Red shouts, and you'd be shouting at a volunteer doing you a favor. And the activity log is the quiet hero: Priya sees the walk happened from another city. That log is the answer to the hole in the brief.

"Everything on this screen exists to answer one question for two different people: did someone handle it? The owner needs to know it happened. The watcher needs credit for it happening."

Present

5 · Your team, through launch 16-20

The exercise · Maps tab: the invite lifecycle state machine, then the 20-screen inventory

"Now, how this works with your team. That one-tap completion looks simple, right? But the second you build it, it starts asking questions. What if two watchers tap the same task? Can you undo after the owner saw it done? So engineering doesn't get a folder of screens from me. They get the state machines, this invite lifecycle, the guard rules, and a screen inventory where all twenty screens are dispositioned with reasons.

QA gets the edge cases during design, not week six. The invite link opened in the wrong account, that's the one a tired human misses at 4pm. Each one becomes an acceptance criterion before build. Product gets one page of truth and a decision log in Notion. The backlog is Linear-ready. Specs live in Figma. And the AI work is logged in Claude, failures included."

Present

6 · What I added, and what I refused 20-22

The exercise · Overview tab: "What the brief leaves open, on purpose"

"Two judgment calls I owe you, both flagged as assumptions in the submission. First, I added something you didn't ask for: completion. The stories never mention it, and I decided the product doesn't work without it, so it went into scope and I defended it in the PRD. Second, I refused things that were easy to add: payments and sitter vetting, because this is trusted people, not Rover. In-app messaging, because these two people already text. That's the Not-Building table. Models are very good at adding. The job is often subtraction."

Present

7 · AI honestly, the findings, and the close 22-25

"You encouraged AI use, so here's the honest ledger. It compressed the reading, the first drafts, the edge-case lists, and the build itself. That's how four hours produced this much. And it was also confidently wrong: its first overdue styling came back red and urgent. Standard pattern, wrong product. The amber correction, and the rest of the misses, are in the AI log.

One more thing, because I think it matters most. The review didn't stop when I hit send. I went back through my own submission with fresh eyes and found real flaws: a stray owner field that breaks the brief's own per-pet rule, a retention metric that punishes an episodic product for working, an emergency vet number I cut by category. The original stays public and unedited. The findings became talking points, not quiet patches.

"That's the practice: a designer standing exactly where the judgment goes, including on their own work. Production got cheap. Deciding didn't. That's the talk. Questions?"

Their Q&A

"We didn't ask for completion. Why add it?"

The exercise · Prototype: tap a checkmark, then the Activity log

"Because the product doesn't work without it. The stories describe a schedule that gets written and read, and nobody can ever mark anything done. Two of the sample tasks are medication, so without completion, the failure mode is a double-dosed animal. Adding to a brief is a risk, so I did it the accountable way: it's in the PRD as a defended decision, with the reasoning. If I'm wrong, there's a paragraph you can argue with."

Their Q&A

"Walk us through the invite flow."

Demo 1 · Prototype: Invite a watcher → Invitation sent → "You're watching Kenji" → Remove access?

"The constraint says invitees already have accounts. So instead of faking onboarding, the app tells the truth: your friend needs an account first, and here's a pre-written message to send them. Revoke is first-class, because the brief promises owners visibility and control. The whole lifecycle is a state machine: invited, accepted, revoked, expired.

"And I'll volunteer the weak spot: this puts work on an anxious owner who's busy packing. First flow I'd test."

Their Q&A

"How would you validate before building?"

Demo 1 · Documents: research plan, the decision rules

"Assumptions get ranked by what it costs to be wrong. And the decision rules get written before the study runs, so I can't rationalize afterward. Top of the list: does completion visibility reassure the owner without making the watcher feel watched? Four of six watchers fine with it, we ship itemized. Three or more not, we ship passive.

"And I do the interviews myself. You don't delegate listening."

Their Q&A

"What ships first?"

Demo 1 · Maps: the 20 screens, point at the prototyped column

"The smallest thing that completes the trust loop. One pet, one watcher, care moments with completion, the activity view. The gate is five real handoffs, real people, real pets. Everything else waits in line behind that, and the Not-Building table says why."

Their Q&A

"Where did your metrics come from?"

Demo 1 · Documents: PRD metrics table, point right at it

"Honestly? Nowhere. That's the flaw in this table. The 60 percent looks measured and it isn't. It should've said working hypotheses, replace after five real handoffs. And the retention number actually punishes the product for working: a 90-day window on a product people use twice a year reads a happy customer as churn. I'd measure 'used again on the next trip' instead."

Their Q&A

"How do you work with PMs?"

"The PM owns the why and the priority. I own making the decision legible. The artifact I care most about is the one page of truth from alignment: one metric, the v1 cut line, the riskiest assumption, an owner for each. If a decision isn't on that page, it didn't happen. Strategy before the build is how you all work too. That page is what a strategy sprint should leave behind."

The exercise · Maps: object map and invite lifecycle

"The test is simple: do they still need me in the room? They shouldn't."

Their Q&A

"What's wrong with your v1?" (own each fast)

Demo 1 · Maps: the object map, point at the two ownership edges

Ownership stored twice: "See these two edges? Both say who owns the pet. And the brief itself says roles are per-pet. My Membership table was the right instinct… the stray owner field betrayed it. I'd derive ownership. If I built it again, I'd derive it, and that's the fix I'd defend."

Privacy fallback vs. safety: "My passive fallback protected the relationship by quietly destroying the double-dosing guarantee. The fix is a third variant, itemized for anything safety-flagged. A fallback needs the same scrutiny as the primary."

Missing emergency vet number: "Cut by association. I excluded 'vet records,' and the emergency number went with it. Needs don't arrive in categories. Now when I cut a category, I ask which single field inside it was doing the real work."

The visual seams (say it before they see it): "And the craft has seams. Count the oranges on this screen: five, and none of them reconcile. The hero stats say two done of seven, one waiting, five more… that's eight. Four hours buys a direction, not a reconciled system. The fix is one accent, one token file, and it's why QA moved upstream in my process. I left it all exactly as I shipped it. An honest baseline is worth more than a quietly polished one."

Words

Acronyms

PRDProduct Requirements Document. The what and the why in one place. Yours has the Not-Building table in it.
ACAcceptance criteria. The testable sentence that defines "done" before anyone builds. You write these with QA during design.
TPMTechnical Product Manager. Goji's delivery-focused PMs. Your backlog IDs are for them, straight into Linear.
WCAG 2.2 AAThe accessibility standard: contrast, keyboard, focus states. Your honest line: built-to-standard is not the same as tested-against-standard.
DTCGDesign Tokens Community Group, the W3C standard JSON format for design tokens, so any tool can read them. Your Figma plugin exports it.
MCPModel Context Protocol. The plumbing that lets an AI read live tools and data. Yours: Claude reads real variables out of a Figma file instead of guessing.
RRULEThe calendar standard for complex repeats, "every other Tuesday." You cut it: real routines are daily or weekly. Revisit with evidence.
CRUDCreate, read, update, delete. The basic data operations. The pet-management stories are plain CRUD, and that's fine.
IAInformation architecture. What lives where, and what it's called.
SPASingle-page app. One page that swaps views without reloading. Your exercise hub is one.
Words

Product and process words

GreenfieldNo existing product, no legacy. Starting clean. Their brief uses this word about PetWatch.
TimeboxA fixed time budget that beats scope. Four hours meant deciding what not to do.
DispositionedEvery screen given a verdict: prototyped, specified, or deferred, with a reason. Your word for the 20-screen inventory. If it lands oddly, say "every screen has a status and a reason."
DeferredCut on purpose, written down, with a trigger that would bring it back. Not forgotten, parked.
Riskiest assumptionThe belief that kills the product if it turns out false. Research goes there first.
Decision ruleThe pass-fail line written before the study runs, so you can't rationalize after. "Four of six watchers fine with it, we ship itemized."
North starThe one metric the whole team steers by. One, not five.
PersonaA sketch of a real user type. Yours: Priya, anxious, packing, short on time.
Swim lanesA flow diagram split into rows by who acts: owner lane, watcher lane, system lane. In your maps page.
Object mapThe nouns of the product and how they connect: User, Pet, Membership, Task. Where your ownership-stored-twice finding lives.
State machineAll the states something can be in, and the only allowed moves between them. Your invite lifecycle: invited, sent, accepted, watching, revoked, expired.
Edge caseRare but real situation. Your line: usually a product decision we haven't made yet.
Optimistic UIThe interface says "done" instantly and syncs in the background; undo covers mistakes. Your one-tap completion.
Derived stateA fact computed from the record, never stored in a second place where it can disagree. Why you'd derive ownership from Membership.
Strategy sprintGoji's term: a short, collaborative push to set direction and surface insight before anything gets built. Your "one page of truth" is what one should leave behind.

Design tokensNamed design values, color, spacing, type, stored once and used everywhere, so a decision is made one time.
